1Why this matters

A claim, a picture, and a promise.

The claim: every quadratic equation can be solved. Some yield to factorising; some need completing the square; some need the formula. Today you learn all three — and a single number that tells you what to expect before you start solving.

That number is the discriminant, b² − 4ac. Its sign predicts the picture below: two crossings, one touch, or none at all.

The promise: by the end of today you'll choose the most efficient method on sight, derive the quadratic formula from completed-square form (so it stops being a memorised mystery), and read the discriminant the way a chess player reads the board — before the move, not after.
The graph of y = ax² + bx + c is a parabola. Its crossings of the x-axis are the real solutions of the equation ax² + bx + c = 0. Three cases — three pictures:

Roots are the navy dots: where the parabola meets the x-axis. The discriminant tells you how many there are — before you solve.

5Three methods, one formula

We'll walk through each method on a clean example, then build the formula from scratch. The derivation is the centrepiece — reveal one step at a time.

Method 1 — Factorising

Solve: x² − 5x + 6 = 0

1
Find two numbers
We want two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to −5. Try −2 and −3: (−2)(−3) = 6 ✓ and (−2) + (−3) = −5 ✓.
2
Factorise
(x − 2)(x − 3) = 0
3
Apply the null factor law
If two things multiply to give 0, at least one of them is 0.
x − 2 = 0   or   x − 3 = 0
⟹ x = 2   or   x = 3
Answerx = 2 or x = 3  ·  two real roots

Method 2 — Completing the square

Solve: x² + 4x − 1 = 0

1
Move the constant across
x² + 4x = 1
2
Add (half of b)² to both sides
Half of 4 is 2. Squared: 4. Add 4 to both sides.
x² + 4x + 4 = 1 + 4
(x + 2)² = 5
3
Square root, then solve
x + 2 = ±√5
x = −2 ± √5
Answerx = −2 + √5  or  x = −2 − √5

Method 3 — Where the quadratic formula comes from

Start: ax² + bx + c = 0  (with a ≠ 0). Goal: solve for x.

1
Divide both sides by a
Get rid of the leading coefficient. We can do this because a ≠ 0.
x² + bax + ca = 0
2
Move ca to the other side
x² + bax = −ca
3
Complete the square — add (b2a)² to both sides
Half the coefficient of x, then square it. We add it to both sides so the equation stays balanced.
x² + bax + b²4a² = −ca + b²4a²
4
Factorise the left side as a perfect square
The reason we added that exact term — now the left becomes a clean square.
(x + b2a)² = b²4a² − ca
5
Common denominator on the right
Rewrite ca as 4ac4a². Now the right side is a single fraction — and look what appears in the numerator.
(x + b2a)² = b² − 4ac4a²
6
Take the square root of both sides
Don't forget the ± — a square root can be positive or negative.
x + b2a = ±√(b² − 4ac)2a
7
Subtract b2a and combine into one fraction
x = −b2a ± √(b² − 4ac)2a
= −b ± √(b² − 4ac)2a
8
There it is — the quadratic formula
The quantity under the square root, b² − 4ac, is the discriminant. It alone tells you whether the ± gives two roots, one, or none. Positive → two; zero → one; negative → no real solutions, because no real number squares to a negative.
The quadratic formulax = −b ± √(b² − 4ac)2a

Method 3 in action — apply the formula

Solve: 2x² + 3x − 4 = 0

1
Identify a, b, c
a = 2,   b = 3,   c = −4
2
Compute the discriminant first
Always do this before substituting — it tells you what to expect.
b² − 4ac = 3² − 4(2)(−4) = 9 + 32 = 41
Positive ⟹ two real roots, both irrational.
3
Substitute into the formula
x = −3 ± √412(2) = −3 ± √414
Answerx = −3 + √414  or  x = −3 − √414

And when the discriminant is negative…

Solve: x² + 2x + 5 = 0

1
Compute the discriminant
b² − 4ac = 2² − 4(1)(5) = 4 − 20 = −16
2
Interpret the sign
The formula would ask us to compute √(−16). No real number squares to give a negative — so there is no real x that satisfies the equation.
The parabola y = x² + 2x + 5 sits entirely above the x-axis — it never crosses.
AnswerNo real solutions.
3
Looking ahead
In Year 11 you'll meet complex numbers, where √(−16) does have a value (4i). For now — and for everything you'll be assessed on this year — "no real solutions" is the answer, and the discriminant told us this before we started.

A quadratic equation has b² − 4ac = −7.   How many real solutions does it have?
Not quite — "two real solutions" needs a positive discriminant. The ± in the formula only gives two distinct real answers when what's under the square root is positive. Here it's −7, which is negative — so the square root step breaks down in the reals. Take another look at the three-parabola picture in Section 1: the left panel is the b²−4ac > 0 case, not this one.
Close idea, wrong sign. A repeated root (one real solution) happens when the discriminant is exactly zero — the parabola just touches the x-axis. With b²−4ac = −7, the parabola sits entirely above or below the axis without touching it at all. Negative discriminant always means no real solutions.
Yes. The formula x = (−b ± √(b²−4ac))/(2a) would have us take √(−7) — and no real number squares to give a negative. Visually, the parabola never crosses the x-axis. Notice: you didn't need to know a, b, or c separately — the discriminant alone settled it.
Actually you have everything you need. That's the whole point of the discriminant: a single number that predicts the number of real solutions without solving. Negative discriminant → no real solutions, every time, no matter what a, b, and c are individually. Re-read Step 8 of the derivation.
