1Why this matters — where two lines meet

A real situation that's secretly today's algebra.

Two food-delivery riders are weighing up jobs. QuickEats pays a $20 flat fee plus $3 per delivery. RideShare pays a $5 fee plus $6 per delivery.

  • QuickEats earnings: y = 20 + 3x
  • RideShare earnings: y = 5 + 6x

The break-even point — where both pay the same — is where the two lines cross. Last lesson you found that point by drawing both lines on the same axes. Today you'll find it algebraically, without a graph, so the numbers stay exact.

💡 The big idea: a simultaneous-equation solution is an ordered pair (x, y) that makes both equations true at the same time. Graphically that's the intersection of the two lines. Today we get to it with algebra.

Solution
Tap to flip
An ordered pair (x, y) that satisfies both equations.
(4, 0) is the solution where the two lines cross.
Substitution
Tap to flip
Replace a variable in one equation with an expression from the other.
If y = 3x − 2, then put 3x − 2 wherever y appears in the other equation.
Elimination
Tap to flip
Add or subtract the equations so that one variable cancels.
In 3x + 2y = 12 and x − 2y = 4, adding cancels the y's.
Coefficient
Tap to flip
The number multiplying a variable. Elimination needs coefficients to match (same or opposite).
In 5x − 3y = 8: the coefficients are 5 and −3.

Two routes to the same crossing point

Substitution and elimination both land at the same (x, y). The choice between them is about which is easiest for the system you've been given.

Substitution

Take an expression for one variable and slot it into the other equation.

Use when one variable is already isolated

Elimination

Add or subtract the two equations so a variable cancels. Scale one or both equations first if the coefficients don't line up.

Use when coefficients line up (or can be made to)

5Worked example — by substitution

Solve simultaneously: y = 3x − 2   and   2x + y = 8

1
Spot what's already isolated
y is already by itself in equation (1). So we can substitute the expression 3x − 2 for y in equation (2).
(1)  y = 3x − 2
(2)  2x + y = 8
→ replace y in (2) with (3x − 2)
2
Solve the one-variable equation
Now there's only x's. Expand, simplify, and solve.
2x + (3x − 2) = 8
5x − 2 = 8
5x = 10
x = 2
3
Back-substitute and verify in both
Put x = 2 back into the simpler equation to find y. Then check the pair satisfies both originals.
y = 3(2) − 2 = 4
Check (1): y = 3(2) − 2 = 4 ✓
Check (2): 2(2) + 4 = 8 ✓
Solution (x, y) = (2, 4)
The algebra and the graph agree: the two lines cross exactly at (2, 4).

6Together — solve 3x + 2y = 12 and x − 2y = 4

Look at the y-coefficients: +2 and −2. They're already opposites — so adding the two equations cancels y. No scaling needed.

Both methods land at the same crossing point — here, on the x-axis at (4, 0).
💡 The elimination move: when one variable's coefficients are already opposites — like +2y and −2y — add the equations and that variable disappears. When they're identical, subtract instead.
