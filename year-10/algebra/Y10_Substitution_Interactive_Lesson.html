1Warm-up — you already substitute every day

A real-life problem first. Algebra second.

Say you work at a local cafe. The pay is $25 an hour.

  • If you work 4 hours, you earn $100.
  • If you work 6 hours, you earn $150.
  • If you work 8 hours, you earn $200.

You just did something powerful — you used a rule ($25 × hours) and put different numbers in. That's substitution.

In algebra we'd write the rule as P = 25h, where P is your pay and h is the hours you work. When we replace h with a number — like h = 6 — that's called substituting. Today we'll do this with all sorts of formulae, especially the ones you'll meet in finance.

4Worked example — the 3-step method

Your weekly pay is given by the formula P = 25h.
Find your pay P when you work h = 8 hours.

1
Identify — what's given, what we need to find
List the values you've been given, and the variable you need to find.
Given: h = 8     Find: P
2
Apply — replace the variable and calculate
Rewrite the formula with the number in place of the letter, then work it out.
P = 25h
P = 25 × 8
P = $200
3
Verify — does the answer make sense?
At $25 per hour, 8 hours should give 8 × $25 = $200. The answer matches the context. ✓
$200 is a reasonable pay for 8 hours of work at $25/hour.
Answer P = $200
WE DO
Let's work one out together
Discuss each step with your neighbour. We'll fill in the answers as a class.

5Together — find the interest using I = PRT100

A friend invests $400 in a savings account at a rate of 5% for 3 years. How much interest do they earn? We'll break this into three small steps. Discuss with the person next to you, then we'll fill in the box together.

💡 Why Identify → Apply → Verify works: finance formulae often have three or four variables. Listing what's given before substituting stops you mixing up the numbers. Verifying catches the daft errors — does $60 of interest on $400 actually sound right? (5% of $400 is $20 per year. Three years. Yep, $60.)
