1Why this matters

A real decision that's secretly today's algebra.

You're choosing between two phone plans:

  • EasyTalk: $4 a month, plus $1 per call
  • PayPerCall: no monthly fee, $3 per call

If you only make one or two calls, PayPerCall is cheaper. If you make twenty, EasyTalk wins. Somewhere in between, the two plans cost exactly the same. That's the break-even point — and it's the point you'd switch providers above.

Both plans can be written as a straight-line equation: y = x + 4 for EasyTalk and y = 3x for PayPerCall. The break-even is where the two lines cross — one point that lies on both lines at once. Today we find these crossings by graphing — and we see why some pairs never cross, and others share every point.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the meaning. Five words you'll use for the rest of the topic.

Simultaneous
equations
Tap to flip
Two (or more) equations being considered together. We want values of the variables that satisfy every equation at once.
y = 2x − 1 and y = −x + 5 are a simultaneous pair.
Solution
Tap to flip
A pair of values (x, y) that makes both equations true at the same time.
For the pair above, the solution is (2, 3).
Intersection
Tap to flip
The point where two graphs cross. That single point lies on both lines — so its coordinates are the solution.
Solving graphically = finding the intersection.
Parallel lines
Tap to flip
Two lines with the same gradient but different y-intercepts. They never meet, so the pair has no solution.
y = 2x + 1 and y = 2x − 3 are parallel.
Coincident lines
Tap to flip
Two equations whose graphs are the same line. Every point on the line is a solution — there are infinitely many.
y = x + 2 and 2y = 2x + 4 are coincident.

💡 Spot the gradient. When both equations are in the form y = mx + c, equal ms mean the lines are either parallel (different c) or coincident (same c). Different ms guarantee they cross.

4The two special cases

Most pairs cross once. Two important pairs don't — and they're easy to miss if you forget to look at the gradients first.

Parallel — no solution

y = 2x + 1  and  y = 2x − 3
Same gradient (2), different intercepts. The lines stay 4 apart forever — they never cross. There's no solution.

Coincident — infinite solutions

y = x + 2  and  2y = 2x + 4
Divide the second by 2: y = x + 2. Same line. Every point on it satisfies both — there are infinitely many solutions.

6Worked example — solve y = 2x − 1 and y = −x + 5 graphically

The graphical method in three steps: plot line 1, plot line 2, read off the crossing.

1
Plot y = 2x − 1
Gradient 2, y-intercept −1. Plot the intercept at (0, −1), then rise 2, run 1.
y-intercept: (0, −1)
Slope: up 2, right 1 → next point (1, 1), then (2, 3), (3, 5)…
2
Plot y = −x + 5 on the same axes
Gradient −1, y-intercept 5. From (0, 5) go down 1, right 1.
y-intercept: (0, 5)
Slope: down 1, right 1 → next point (1, 4), then (2, 3), (3, 2)…
3
Read off the intersection
The two lines cross at one point. Drop perpendiculars to read its coordinates.
The lines cross at (2, 3).
Check: in line 1, 2(2) − 1 = 3 ✓. In line 2, −(2) + 5 = 3 ✓.
Solution x = 2, y = 3  or  (2, 3)
