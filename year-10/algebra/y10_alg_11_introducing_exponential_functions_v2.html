1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's algebra.

Suppose you invest $1000 in a savings account paying 8% interest per year. Watch how the balance grows:

  • After 1 year: $1080.00
  • After 2 years: $1166.40
  • After 3 years: $1259.71
  • After 4 years: $1360.49

The amount you gain each year keeps getting bigger — but each year's balance is exactly 1.08 times the previous year's. The same multiplier, applied over and over.

That repeated multiplication is the heart of an exponential function. Whenever a quantity is multiplied by a fixed factor at each step — savings, populations, radioactive decay, viral growth — the relationship is exponential. Today you'll learn how to recognise one from a table, contrast it with linear and quadratic patterns, and read the family of curves y = ax.

Tap to flip
The fixed positive number that's multiplied repeatedly. In y = ax the base is a.
In y = 5x, the base is 5.
Common ratio
Tap to flip
The constant factor between consecutive terms in an exponential sequence. Found by dividing any term by the one before.
In 3, 6, 12, 24: ratio = 2.
Growth
Tap to flip
When the base is greater than 1, values increase as x increases. The curve sweeps upward.
y = 2x doubles every step.
Decay
Tap to flip
When the base is between 0 and 1, values decrease toward zero. The curve sweeps downward and flattens.
y = (12)x halves every step.

4The family y = ax

Four exponential graphs side by side. Bases above 1 grow; bases between 0 and 1 decay. Every one passes through (0, 1).

The four members of the family y = ax plotted on the same axes. Warm colours: growth (base > 1). Cool colours: decay (0 < base < 1).
y = 3x — growth, fastest
y = 2x — growth
y = (12)x — decay
y = (13)x — decay, fastest
💡 Three things to notice. (1) Every curve passes through (0, 1) — that's because a0 = 1 for any positive base. (2) Bigger base ⇒ steeper growth (or steeper decay if the base is a small fraction). (3) The x-axis is a horizontal asymptote: the curves get arbitrarily close to y = 0 without ever touching it.

5Worked example — is this table exponential?

A table of x, y values. Use the differences-then-ratios routine to identify the family.

x01234
y36122448
1
Test for linear — first differences
Subtract each y from the next. If the differences are constant, the table is linear.
6 − 3 = 3  |  12 − 6 = 6  |  24 − 12 = 12  |  48 − 24 = 24
First differences: 3, 6, 12, 24 — not constant, so it is not linear.
2
Test for quadratic — second differences
Take the differences of the differences. If those are constant, the table is quadratic.
6 − 3 = 3  |  12 − 6 = 6  |  24 − 12 = 12
Second differences: 3, 6, 12 — not constant, so it is not quadratic.
3
Test for exponential — ratios
Divide each y by the previous one. If the ratios are constant, the table is exponential — and that constant is the common ratio.
6 ÷ 3 = 2  |  12 ÷ 6 = 2  |  24 ÷ 12 = 2  |  48 ÷ 24 = 2
Common ratio: 2. Each step multiplies by 2. Exponential.
Conclusion Exponential with common ratio r = 2. The rule is y = 3 × 2x.
💡 The recognition routine: differences → second differences → ratios. You stop at the first test that gives a constant — that constant tells you both the family and a number that defines the relation (slope, second-difference, or common ratio).

Apply the routine from the worked example to this new table. Pick the option that matches.

Not linear — first differences aren't constant. The first differences are 12 − 4 = 8, 36 − 12 = 24, 108 − 36 = 72. They change — and they change by being multiplied by 3 each time. That last observation is the giveaway: when first differences grow by a fixed factor, the table itself is exponential, not linear.
Close diagnosis — but you stopped at step 2. Quadratic requires constant second differences. Here the second differences are 24 − 8 = 16 and 72 − 24 = 48 — not equal. Run the next test: ratios. 12 ÷ 4 = 3, 36 ÷ 12 = 3, 108 ÷ 36 = 3. Constant ratio = 3 → exponential.
Correct. Each y is exactly 3 times the previous one — the common ratio is 3. Because the rule multiplies by a fixed factor at each step, the table is exponential. (A possible rule: y = 43 × 3x.)
Don't give up at the differences stage. The first two tests (1st and 2nd differences) both fail, but the third test passes cleanly: try 12 ÷ 4 = 3, 36 ÷ 12 = 3, 108 ÷ 36 = 3. A constant ratio is the exponential signal — this table is exponential, not "none".
