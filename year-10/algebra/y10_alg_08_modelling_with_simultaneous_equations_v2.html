1Why this matters

A real decision that's secretly today's algebra.

Two electricians have quoted you to rewire a shed:

  • EzyClean charges a $50 call-out fee plus $35 an hour.
  • FreshCo charges only $20 to come out — but $50 an hour.

For a quick job, FreshCo looks cheaper. For a long job, EzyClean looks cheaper. At what point does the swap happen? That moment — where the two costs are exactly equal — is the break-even point.

Real decisions — which quote to accept, when one provider becomes cheaper than another, how many cups of lemonade you need to sell to stop losing money — all come down to finding and interpreting a break-even point. Today we'll model these situations with a pair of equations, solve them, and explain the answer in everyday language.

Simultaneous equations
Tap to flip
Two or more equations that must all be true at the same time. The solution satisfies every one.
If C = 50 + 35h and C = 20 + 50h, find the (h, C) that fits both.

The value where two quantities become equal — two costs match, revenue equals cost, supply meets demand.

3Investigation — strategic reasoning in Weme

A First Nations children's game whose strategic logic is the same logic we use to solve simultaneous equations.

First Nations · Warlpiri Peoples

Weme — a Warlpiri stones game

Weme is a two-player game played by Warlpiri children of the central Australian desert. Pieces are small stones laid out on lines scratched into the sand. Players take turns moving a stone along the lines, capturing an opponent's stone when one of their own pieces sits across from it. Versions are played by First Nations communities across the continent, with the lines, counts, and capture rules adjusted by local Knowledge Holders.

The mathematics in Weme is not in the counting — it's in the simultaneous thinking. A good player must hold several conditions in mind at once: where can I move, where might my opponent move next, which of my moves blocks their next move while opening one of mine. Every move has to satisfy multiple constraints at the same time.

The link to today's lesson: when we solve a pair of simultaneous equations, we are doing the same kind of thinking — looking for one answer that satisfies both conditions at once. The Weme player thinks in positions; we think in numbers. The reasoning is the same: don't satisfy one constraint and forget the other.

First Nations Knowledge Holders are the authority on the cultural meaning, rules, and significance of these games. The mathematical link drawn here is one way Australian classrooms can recognise the deep strategic reasoning developed through traditional instructive games. Source for game description: SA Curriculum (Mathematics R-10 Prototype 2) cultural responsiveness guidance.

5Worked example — which electrician should I hire?

EzyClean charges $50 call-out + $35 per hour. FreshCo charges $20 call-out + $50 per hour. For what length of job do they cost the same — and which is cheaper outside that?

1
Define variables and write the two equations
Let h = hours of work, and C = total cost in dollars.
CEzyClean = 50 + 35h
CFreshCo = 20 + 50h
2
Set the costs equal and solve
The break-even point is where the two costs are the same. Set CEzyClean = CFreshCo.
50 + 35h = 20 + 50h
50 − 20 = 50h − 35h
30 = 15h
h = 2 hours
Substitute back: C = 50 + 35(2) = $120
3
Read the graph and answer the question in everyday language
Plot both lines. The intersection is the break-even point. Which line is lower on each side tells us who is cheaper.
In everyday language: if the job takes less than 2 hours, choose FreshCo — the lower call-out wins. If the job takes more than 2 hours, choose EzyClean — the lower hourly rate catches up. At exactly 2 hours they tie at $120.
Answer Break-even at h = 2 hours, both costing $120. Below 2 hours → FreshCo. Above 2 hours → EzyClean.

6Together — when does the lemonade stand break even?

You're running a lemonade stand at the school fete. Setup cost is $80 (sign, jug, lemons, sugar). You sell each cup for $6; each cup costs you $2 in ingredients. How many cups do you need to sell before you stop losing money?

💡 Why this works: revenue and cost are both linear in the number of cups sold. The break-even point is where the cost line and the revenue line cross — below it, you've spent more than you've earned; above it, you start making profit. Same logic as the two-electricians problem, just dressed in different clothes.
