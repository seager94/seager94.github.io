1Why this matters

A familiar shape, hiding a useful trick.

A football is kicked into the air. Its height (in metres) t seconds after the kick is modelled by

h(t) = −t2 + 8t + 5.

How high does the ball go, and when does it peak? Right now, the expression hides both answers. But if we could rewrite it as

h(t) = −(t − 4)2 + 21,

we could read off both straight away: a maximum height of 21 m at t = 4 s.

That rewrite is called completing the square. It's the lesson that unlocks the turning point of any parabola — and the manipulation you'll use to derive the quadratic formula next lesson. Get this routine fluent today and the rest of the unit gets much easier.

5Worked example 1 — the routine

Rewrite x2 + 6x + 5 in completed-square form and state the turning point of the related parabola.

1
Halve the coefficient of x
Take the middle coefficient (here, 6), halve it, and that's the number that goes inside the bracket.
6 ÷ 2 = 3, so the bracket is (x + 3)2.
2
Expand the bracket, then adjust the constant
Expanding gives an extra "+ 9" that wasn't in the original. We started with "+ 5", so we have to subtract 4 to fix the constant.
(x + 3)2 = x2 + 6x + 9
Original constant is 5, so subtract 9 − 5 = 4:
x2 + 6x + 5 = (x + 3)2 − 4
3
Read off the turning point
In vertex form (x − h)2 + k, the turning point is (h, k). Here the bracket is (x + 3), which equals (x − (−3)), so h = −3 and k = −4.
y = (x + 3)2 − 4
Turning point: (−3, −4) — a minimum (parabola opens up).
Answer (x + 3)2 − 4  ·  TP at (−3, −4)
