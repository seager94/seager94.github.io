1Why this matters

Two friends invest the same $5000 the same day. Five years later they own very different balances.

Imagine you and a friend both deposit $5000 into a savings account paying 6% per year.

  • Your account pays simple interest — the bank pays 6% of the original $5000 each year ($300).
  • Your friend's account pays compound interest — each year the bank pays 6% of the current balance, so the interest itself starts earning interest.

After 5 years, you have $6500. Your friend has $6691.13. After 30 years the gap blows out to about $5000.

Compound interest is exponential because each period earns interest on the previous interest. Today we use the formula A = P(1 + rn)nt to model how an investment grows over time, and we compare with simple interest to see why "compounding more often" is worth chasing.

Annual interest rate
Tap to flip
The yearly rate the bank pays (or charges), written as a decimal in the formula. Symbol: r.
A rate of 6% p.a. becomes r = 0.06 in the formula.
Compounding frequency
Tap to flip
How many times per year interest is added. Symbol: n.
Annually: n = 1. Monthly: n = 12. Daily: n = 365.
Compound interest
Tap to flip
Interest paid on the principal + previous interest. Balance grows exponentially.
Year 1: 6% of $5000 = $300. Year 2: 6% of $5300 = $318. The base keeps growing.
Simple interest
Tap to flip
Interest paid only on the original principal. Balance grows linearly.
6% of $5000 = $300 every year, no matter what the balance is.

5Worked example 1 — fully worked

Scenario: Lina invests $5000 in a Commonwealth Bank online saver paying 6% per annum compounded annually. What is the balance after 5 years, and how does it compare with simple interest?

Compound interest formula
A = P(1 + rn)nt
P principal · r annual rate (decimal) · n compounds per year · t years
1
Identify the four values
Pull the numbers out of the scenario and convert the percentage to a decimal.
P = 5000  ·  r = 0.06  ·  n = 1 (compounded annually)  ·  t = 5
2
Substitute into the formula
Replace each letter with its value. The base (1 + r/n) becomes 1.06 and the exponent nt becomes 5.
A = 5000 × (1 + 0.061)1×5 = 5000 × (1.06)5
3
Calculate the power and multiply
Type 1.06 ^ 5 into your calculator first, then multiply by the principal.
(1.06)5 = 1.33823 (5 dp)
A = 5000 × 1.33823 = $6691.13
4
Compare with simple interest
If the same $5000 earned simple interest at 6% for 5 years: I = Prt = 5000 × 0.06 × 5 = $1500, so the balance would be $6500.
Compound: $6691.13  ·  Simple: $6500  ·  Extra from compounding: $191.13
Answer A = $6691.13 — compound beats simple by $191.13 over 5 years
Figure 1 · Lina's $5000 over 5 years — compound (solid) vs simple interest (dashed). The gap widens each year because the compound balance earns interest on its own interest.

💡 Inflation reality check. The Reserve Bank of Australia targets inflation of 2–3% per year. If your investment grows at 6% but inflation is 3%, your real return is only about 3% — your buying power grows by less than the headline number suggests. Real return ≈ nominal rate − inflation rate. Anything below inflation means you're going backwards in real terms.
