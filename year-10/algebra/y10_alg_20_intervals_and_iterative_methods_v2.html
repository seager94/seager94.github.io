1Why this matters

Sometimes the answer can't be written as a tidy fraction or surd. You have to close in on it.

A friend buys a share at $50. Each year it grows by 8%. They want to know when it first passes $80.

There's no neat answer like "year 5" — the value follows 50 × 1.08n, and the moment it crosses $80 lands between two whole years. So you do what mathematicians have done for centuries: you trap the answer between two values you can compute, then close the gap.

Today: the same idea applied to a curve. When a quadratic has roots that aren't whole numbers, the bisection method traps each root between two x-values where the function changes sign — then halves the interval, again and again, until you've cornered the answer. The same sign-analysis tells you where the quadratic is positive and where it's negative.

Root (zero)
Tap to flip
A value of x where f(x) = 0 — where the graph crosses the x-axis.
For f(x) = x² − 9, the roots are x = 3 and x = −3.
Interval
Tap to flip
A range of x-values, written like (a, b). The bisection method works inside an interval.
The interval (2, 3) means every x between 2 and 3.
Sign change
Tap to flip
When f goes from positive to negative (or back) across an interval. A continuous curve that changes sign must have a root in between.
If f(2) = −5 and f(3) = +2, the signs differ — so a root lies in (2, 3).
Midpoint
Tap to flip
The exact centre of an interval: a + b2.
The midpoint of (2, 3) is 2.5.
Bisection
Tap to flip
An iterative method: cut the interval in half, keep the half where the sign change still happens, and repeat.
Each step halves the width — after 4 steps an interval of width 1 has shrunk to 0.0625.

5Worked example — bisection on f(x) = 2x² − 3x − 7

From the graph in Section 3, the positive root sits somewhere in (2, 3). Let's close in.

1
Confirm a sign change in the starting interval
Evaluate f at both endpoints. If the signs differ, the curve must cross zero somewhere inside.
f(2) = 2(2)² − 3(2) − 7 = 8 − 6 − 7 = −5  (negative)
f(3) = 2(3)² − 3(3) − 7 = 18 − 9 − 7 = +2  (positive)
Signs differ ✓ — a root lies in (2, 3).
2
Halve the interval — step 1
Midpoint m = (2 + 3)/2 = 2.5. Evaluate and check which half still has the sign change.
f(2.5) = 2(6.25) − 7.5 − 7 = 12.5 − 14.5 = −2  (negative)
The sign change is now between 2.5 (−) and 3 (+).
New interval: (2.5, 3)  — width 0.5.
3
Halve again — step 2
Midpoint m = (2.5 + 3)/2 = 2.75. Evaluate and keep the half with the sign change.
f(2.75) = 2(7.5625) − 8.25 − 7 = 15.125 − 15.25 = −0.125  (negative)
The sign change is now between 2.75 (−) and 3 (+).
New interval: (2.75, 3)  — width 0.25.

6Order the bisection routine

These six cards describe one full bisection iteration — but they're in the wrong order. Use the ↑ and ↓ buttons to put them right, then tap Check order. Hint: the routine starts before you have any midpoint, and ends ready to start over.

Routine: one bisection iteration on a given interval (a, b)
  • Evaluate f(m) at the midpoint.
  • Replace the endpoint with m and repeat from step 1.
  • Evaluate f(a) and f(b) at the endpoints.
  • Decide which half — (a, m) or (m, b) — still has the sign change.
  • Confirm the signs of f(a) and f(b) differ.
  • Compute the midpoint m = (a + b)/2.
💡 Why this order matters: if you compute the midpoint before checking signs differ, you might be bisecting an interval with no root in it. Sign-difference is the licence to halve.
