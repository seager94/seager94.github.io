1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's algebra.

A small balance in a savings account grows by a constant factor each year — that's compound interest. If $1 doubles every year, you have $2, then $4, then $8, then $16. Each year is a power of 2.

A question your bank might ask: "After how many years will your balance reach $1024?" You're not multiplying — you're looking for an exponent. That's an exponential equation.

The same kind of question shows up everywhere money compounds, populations grow, or radioactive material decays. Today's tool: when both sides of 2x = 1024 can be written as powers of the same base, the exponents must be equal. The exponential equation reduces to a one-line linear solve.

Same answer, seen graphically

To solve 2x = 8 graphically, plot y = 2x and the horizontal line y = 8. The two graphs cross at exactly one point. The x-coordinate of that crossing is your answer.

Here the curve crosses y = 8 at (3, 8), so x = 3 — which is the same answer you get by rewriting 8 = 23 and equating exponents. The algebra and the graph agree because y = 2x is one-to-one: each y-value corresponds to a single x.

6Together — solve 4x = 8

The two sides aren't powers of the same base yet — 4 and 8 are both powers of 2, but neither is a power of the other. We'll choose base 2, rewrite both sides, then equate exponents. Three small steps.

💡 Why this method works: if 2A = 2B then A = B — because y = 2x is one-to-one. Once both sides share a base, the exponential problem becomes a linear one.
