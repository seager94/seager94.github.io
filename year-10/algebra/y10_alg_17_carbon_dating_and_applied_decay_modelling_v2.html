1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's algebra.

How do scientists know that a piece of ancient charcoal from a fireplace is 10,000 years old, or that a wooden tool is 2,500 years old?

They use carbon-14 dating. Every living thing — a tree, a kangaroo, you — takes in tiny amounts of a radioactive form of carbon called carbon-14. When the organism dies, it stops taking in carbon-14, and what's already there decays on a fixed schedule: every 5,730 years, half of it is gone.

So if you measure how much carbon-14 is left in an old piece of wood, you can work backwards to its age. The fraction remaining tells the age.

The exponential decay model lets us read the past from a number. The exponential growth model — same family of curves — also lets us forecast a population's future. But growth models that fit early data can mislead us once a population approaches the limits of its Country/Place. Today we model both, and learn to spot the limit.
A note on respect. Carbon dating of organic materials (such as charcoal from ancient hearths) is conducted by archaeologists in partnership with First Nations Australian Traditional Owners and Elders, who hold the cultural authority over sites and material culture on Country. Carbon dating measures, but it does not replace, the deep time knowledge that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples hold of their own histories — knowledge stretching back tens of thousands of years.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. You'll use these five words constantly today.

Exponential decay
Tap to flip
A quantity that multiplies by a fraction less than 1 at each fixed time step — so it shrinks faster at first, then more slowly.
Carbon-14 multiplies by ½ every 5,730 years.
Exponential growth
Tap to flip
A quantity that multiplies by a number greater than 1 at each fixed time step — so it grows faster and faster.
A colony of 100 quokkas growing 5% per year multiplies by 1.05 each year.
Half-life
Tap to flip
The time it takes for half of a decaying substance to be gone. The next half-life takes the same time again.
Half-life of carbon-14 is 5,730 years.
Growth/decay factor
Tap to flip
The number you multiply by each time step. Above 1 means growth; below 1 means decay.
A 20% yearly rise means factor 1.20. Halving means factor 0.5.
Carrying capacity
Tap to flip
The maximum population the environment of a Country/Place can support, written K. Once the population approaches K, the exponential model overestimates.
A fenced reserve might support K = 400 bilbies — no more.

5Worked example — dating an artefact from its carbon-14

Problem. A piece of charcoal from an ancient hearth is found to contain only 14 of the carbon-14 that fresh wood from the same plant species contains. Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5,730 years. How old is the charcoal?

1
Write the model
Let N be the carbon-14 remaining and N₀ the original. Each half-life multiplies by ½.
N = N₀ × 12n  where n = number of half-lives.
2
Find how many half-lives have passed
Only ¼ of the carbon-14 remains, so we need (½)n = ¼.
12n = 14 = 122
⇒ n = 2 half-lives
3
Multiply by the half-life length to get the age
Each half-life is 5,730 years, so 2 of them gives the age.
Age = n × T½ = 2 × 5,730 = 11,460 years
Answer The charcoal is about 11,460 years old.

6Together — modelling a quokka reintroduction

A fenced reserve on Country has 100 quokkas reintroduced. In the early years the population grows at a steady 20% per year. The reserve can ecologically support a maximum of K = 400 quokkas (its carrying capacity). We'll model the early growth, then critique where the model breaks.

💡 What the figure tells us: the exponential model fits beautifully for the first few years. By year 6 or 7 the real population starts to flatten — food, water and territory are finite, and births slow as the colony fills its range. By year 10, the exponential predicts about 619 quokkas; the carrying capacity is only 400. The model has stopped describing reality.
