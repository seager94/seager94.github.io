1Warm-up — area of a rectangle

Before we deal with brackets, think about something you've known since primary school.

A rectangle has length 5 and width 3. Its area is 5 × 3 = 15. Easy.

Now split the length into 2 + 3 and the width into 1 + 2. The big rectangle is now made of four smaller rectangles:

  • 2 × 1 = 2
  • 3 × 1 = 3
  • 2 × 2 = 4
  • 3 × 2 = 6

Add them up: 2 + 3 + 4 + 6 = 15 ✓ — same answer.

💡 Today's idea: the same trick works when the side lengths are algebraic. A rectangle of length (x + 2) by width (x + 3) can be split into four sub-rectangles. Adding the four areas is expanding the brackets.

The shortcut — FOIL

Each of the four areas in the rectangle is one product of FOIL. First × First, Outer × Outer, Inner × Inner, Last × Last.

Fx · x = x2
Ox · 3 = 3x
I2 · x = 2x
L2 · 3 = 6

Same four products, no rectangle drawn. Use the model to understand; use FOIL to work faster.

Methods preview · perfect squares

(1 + r)2 and why bankers approximate

Try expanding (1 + r)2 = (1 + r)(1 + r) using the area model. You should get:

(1 + r)2 = 1 + 2r + r2

So what? Imagine r is an interest rate written as a decimal — say r = 0.05 (5%).

  • 2r = 0.10 (significant — this is the "simple interest" amount)
  • r2 = 0.0025 (tiny — only a quarter of one percent)

When r is small, r2 is negligible. That's why simple interest ≈ compound interest over short time periods — the cross term 2r dominates, and r2 can be ignored to a good approximation.

You'll meet this idea again in Year 12 Methods as linear approximation — and again in Specialist Maths with binomial series. The seed is here: expand a perfect square, then ignore the small term.
