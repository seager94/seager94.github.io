1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's algebra.

Lana has saved $150 for term-holiday outings. She wants to mix two things:

  • 🎬 Movie trips — $12 each
  • ⛸ Ice-skating sessions — $21 each

If she goes to m movies and s skating sessions, the total cost is 12m + 21s. She can't spend more than $150, and she can't book a negative number of outings. So her choices must satisfy three inequalities at once.

Plotting all three inequalities together on a grid leaves a feasible region — the area that satisfies every rule. The valid plans for Lana are the whole-number points inside it. Today we learn how to draw the region and read the answers off the graph.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. You'll use these five words constantly today.

Linear inequality
Tap to flip
A linear statement using ≤, <, ≥, > instead of =. Its graph is a half-plane.
12m + 21s ≤ 150
Boundary line
Tap to flip
The line you'd get if the inequality were =. Draw it solid for ≤/≥ and dashed for </>.
12m + 21s ≤ 150 → solid boundary 12m + 21s = 150.
Feasible region
Tap to flip
The overlap of all the shaded half-planes — where every inequality is true at once.
In a budget problem, it's where every spending rule is satisfied.
Lattice point
Tap to flip
A point where both coordinates are whole numbers — the only kind that can represent a real-world count.
(3, 4) is a lattice point; (2.5, 1) is not.
System of inequalities
Tap to flip
Two or more inequalities considered together. A solution must satisfy every one of them.
12m+21s≤150, m≥0, s≥0 — three inequalities, one system.

5Worked example — Lana's $150 entertainment budget

System: 12m + 21s ≤ 150, m ≥ 0, s ≥ 0. m = movies at $12, s = skating sessions at $21.

1
Draw the boundary lines and shade the feasible region
Each inequality has a solid boundary (all use ≤ or ≥). The region is in the first quadrant, under the budget line.

Reading the graph

Solid line: budget exactly $150 — included.
Shaded triangle: every (m, s) here costs ≤ $150 with no negatives.
Coral dots: whole-number plans — only these are real choices.
2
List every lattice point in the region — row by row
For each value of s from 0 upward, find the largest m that still satisfies 12m ≤ 150 − 21s.
s = 0: 12m ≤ 150 → m = 0, 1, 2, …, 12   (13 plans)
s = 1: 12m ≤ 129 → m = 0, 1, …, 10   (11 plans)
s = 2: 12m ≤ 108 → m = 0, 1, …, 9   (10 plans)
s = 3: 12m ≤ 87 → m = 0, 1, …, 7   (8 plans)
s = 4: 12m ≤ 66 → m = 0, 1, …, 5   (6 plans)
s = 5: 12m ≤ 45 → m = 0, 1, 2, 3   (4 plans)
s = 6: 12m ≤ 24 → m = 0, 1, 2   (3 plans)
s = 7: 12m ≤ 3 → m = 0   (1 plan)
Total whole-number plans 13 + 11 + 10 + 8 + 6 + 4 + 3 + 1 = 56
3
Interpret a solution in context
A lattice point isn't just a dot — it's a real plan. Pick (m = 9, s = 2) and translate it back into dollars and outings.
Cost = 12 × 9 + 21 × 2 = 108 + 42 = $150
Budget left over = 150 − 150 = $0
Plan: Lana sees 9 movies and goes skating 2 times, spending her budget exactly.
Key idea The feasible region holds every possible plan; whole-number points are the real choices; budget lines passing through a lattice point are the plans that spend it all.

6Together — Bria's $24 café budget

Bria has $24 to spend at the café. Coffees are $4 each and snacks are $3 each. The café requires at least 1 coffee and at least 1 snack. System: 4c + 3n ≤ 24, c ≥ 1, n ≥ 1.

Region notes

Three solid boundaries — all three include their equality.
Triangle is the overlap: budget held AND at least 1 of each.
