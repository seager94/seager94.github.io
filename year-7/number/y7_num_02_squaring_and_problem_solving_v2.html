1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's number sense.

Your school is repaving a square play area in the yard. The principal has measured the side: 23 metres. To order the right amount of paving, they need the area in square metres.

You reach for your calculator — but it's flat. The principal turns to you and says: "You're in Year 7. Can you work out 23 × 23 in your head?"

By the end of today's lesson, you'll do it in under ten seconds — using a picture of a square, not a pen.

Last lesson you learned that 12² = 144 and 20² = 400 by memory. But 23² isn't on the times-table chart. Today we break 23 into 20 + 3, picture the square as four pieces, and add them up: 23² = 400 + 60 + 60 + 9 = 529. Same trick works for any two-digit square.

5Worked example — find 23²

Think of 23² as the area of a 23-by-23 square. We split the side into 20 + 3 and see four pieces.

1
Decompose the side: 23 = 20 + 3
Split each side of the square into a "20" piece and a "3" piece. This cuts the square into four rectangles.
23² = (20 + 3)² = (20 + 3) × (20 + 3)
2
Find the area of each of the four pieces

6Together — find 15²

Decompose 15 as 10 + 5. We'll fill in each piece together — the big 10 × 10 square, the two strips, and the corner — then add them up.

💡 Why two strips? The square has a top strip AND a left strip — both have the same area. That's why the area model always gives two rectangles of the same size, plus one big square and one corner.
