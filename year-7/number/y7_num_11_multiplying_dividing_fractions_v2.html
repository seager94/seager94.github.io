1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's maths.

A jumper marked $60 has 13 off in a sale. How much do you save?

You need to find 13 of $60. That's a multiplication: 13 × 60 = 20. The discount is $20, so the jumper now costs $40.

Notice — multiplying by 13 made the answer smaller, not bigger.

Today we'll multiply (and divide) fractions and decimals — and we'll discover something that surprises a lot of students: multiplying by a fraction less than 1 always makes the answer smaller. We'll see why using a rectangle model before we touch any procedure.

5Worked example — 12 × 13 with a rectangle

First we'll see what 12 × 13 means with a rectangle. Then we'll write the rule.

1
Shade 13 of the rectangle
Split the whole rectangle into 3 equal vertical strips and shade ONE strip — that's 13.
1 strip out of 3 is shaded coral = 13.
2
Now take 12 of that 13
Cut the rectangle horizontally in half. The overlap of the coral 13 and the new top half is the answer: 1 small cell out of 6 equal cells.
1 cell shaded out of 6 total cells = 16. So 12 × 13 = 16.
3
Now the procedure — multiply tops, multiply bottoms
The rectangle showed us why: cutting into 2 then 3 makes 2 × 3 = 6 cells (denominators multiply). 1 of them is the answer (1 × 1 = 1, numerators multiply).
12 × 13 = 1 × 12 × 3 = 16
Rule ab × cd = a × cb × d
💡 Bridge to decimals: the rule works for decimals too — just write each decimal as a fraction over 10 (or 100). For example 0.4 × 0.3 = 410 × 310 = 12100 = 0.12. The two decimal places in the question (1 + 1) become two decimal places in the answer.

7Dividing fractions — how many halves fit in 3?

Division asks "how many of these fit into that?" Let's see the answer first, then learn the shortcut.

1
Start with 3 whole rectangles
Each square below is 1 whole. We have 3 of them. The question is: how many halves fit inside?
3 wholes.
2
Cut each whole into halves — count them
Each whole splits into 2 halves. Three wholes × 2 halves each = 6 halves in total. So 3 ÷ 12 = 6.
6 halves fit in 3 wholes. So 3 ÷ 12 = 6.
3
Now the shortcut — Keep, Change, Flip
Once you've seen the picture, here's the speed rule: Keep the first number, Change ÷ to ×, Flip the second fraction (use its reciprocal).
3 ÷ 12 = 3 × 21 = 3 × 21 × 1 = 61 = 6
Rule (after the picture!) ab ÷ cd = ab × dc
💡 Why does flipping work? Dividing by 12 is asking "how many halves fit?" — which is the same as multiplying by 2. Dividing by 13 asks "how many thirds fit?" — same as multiplying by 3. The reciprocal does this automatically.

For decimals: 6 ÷ 0.5 asks "how many halves in 6?" → 12. Or write 0.5 = 12 and use Keep-Change-Flip.
