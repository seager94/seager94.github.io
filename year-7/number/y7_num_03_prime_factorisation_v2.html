1Why this matters

Every composite number has a single, unique recipe of primes — a kind of mathematical fingerprint.

Numbers like 12, 48 and 72 look unrelated. But hidden inside each one is a recipe of prime numbers that you can't change:

  • 12 = 2 × 2 × 3
  • 48 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3
  • 72 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3

Notice how the recipes share ingredients. That sharing is what makes HCF, LCM, fractions and even cryptography work — and it all starts with finding the primes.

💡 The big idea: every composite number can be written as a product of primes in exactly one way (the order doesn't matter). Today we'll find those recipes using a tool called the factor tree, and write them neatly using exponents: 48 = 24 × 3 instead of 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3.

A whole number greater than 1 that has more than two factors — it can be split into a product of smaller numbers.

Factor

A number that divides evenly into another with no remainder.

Factor tree
Tap to flip

Exponent

A small raised number that tells you how many times to multiply the base by itself.
24 means 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16.

5Worked example — factorise 48

Write 48 as a product of prime numbers using exponent notation.

1
Pick any factor pair to start the tree
48 = 6 × 8. Either factor could be split further if it's not prime. (You could have started with 4 × 12 or 2 × 24 — the recipe at the bottom turns out the same.)
composite (split again) prime (stop here)
2
Keep splitting any composite until only primes remain
6 = 2 × 3 (both prime — stop). 8 = 2 × 4, but 4 is composite, so split again: 4 = 2 × 2. Every branch must end at a prime.
Primes at the bottom of the tree: 2, 3, 2, 2, 2.
3
Collect the primes — write repeats using exponents
Count how many of each prime appear. There are four 2's and one 3. Write the answer with primes in ascending order.
48  =  2 × 3 × 2 × 2 × 2
     =  2 × 2 × 2 × 2  ×  3
     =  24  ×  3
Answer 48 = 24 × 3

💡 Why factor trees work: no matter which factor pair you start with (8 × 9, or 6 × 12, or 2 × 36), every branch keeps splitting until you hit primes — and the list of primes at the bottom is always the same. That's the "Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic", and it's the reason we trust prime factorisation as a unique fingerprint.
