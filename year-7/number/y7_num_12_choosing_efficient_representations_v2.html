1Why this matters

Same answer, different effort — the smart choice is to pick the form that gets you there fastest.

You're at a sale. A $60 jumper is marked one-third off. How much do you save?

You could work it out three different ways:

  • As a fraction: 13 of 60 = 20 ✓ (fast!)
  • As a decimal: 0.333… × 60 = … 😵
  • As a percentage: 33.3% of 60 = … 😵

The fraction form gets you to $20 in one step. The other forms make the same problem messy.

Today you'll already know how to add, multiply and convert fractions, decimals and percentages — that's not new. What's new is the strategic choice: looking at the numbers, deciding which form is fastest, and only then doing the maths.

5Worked example — calculate 25% of 64

Watch how I think before I calculate. The decision about which form to use is the new bit.

My 4-step strategy
  1. Look at the numbers.
  2. Decide which form is fastest — fraction, decimal, or percentage.
  3. Convert if needed.
  4. Calculate.
1
Look and decide — which form is fastest?
I see 25% and 64. I know 25% has three equivalent forms: 14, 0.25, and 25%. Now I check the other number. Is 64 divisible by 4? Yes — 64 ÷ 4 = 16, no remainder. So a fraction will be one quick division. If I went with 0.25 × 64, that's two-digit multiplication — slower.
25% of 64   →   choose 14 because 64 ÷ 4 is clean.
2
Convert
Change 25% into its fraction equivalent.
25% = 25100 = 14
3
Calculate
Now the arithmetic is easy: just divide 64 by 4.
14 of 64  =  64 ÷ 4  =  16
Answer 25% of 64 = 16

6Together — calculate 75% of 32

We'll walk through the same strategy: look, decide, convert, calculate.

Today's strategy — applied here
  1. Look: the numbers are 75% and 32.
  2. Decide: is 32 divisible by anything friendly? Yes — by 4. And 75% = 34. So fraction looks fast.
  3. Convert 75% into a fraction.
  4. Calculate using the fraction.
💡 Why we do it this way: the numbers in the question tell you which form is fastest. You're not memorising rules — you're reading the numbers and reacting to them. When you tackle the "You do" practice next, ask yourself "look and decide" before you start calculating.
