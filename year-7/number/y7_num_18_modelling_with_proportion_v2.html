1Why this matters

A real situation, and the modelling cycle we'll use all lesson.

Your school's attendance officer has to send Monday's numbers to head office. They could send:

  • "378 students attended out of 420 enrolled", or
  • "90% of students attended on Monday", or
  • "For every 10 students enrolled, 9 turned up."

All three are correct. But head office reads dozens of these — the percentage is fastest to compare to last week and to other schools. Choosing the right way to express a number is the key skill of today's lesson.

The modelling cycle in plain language:
  1. Read the situation.
  2. Decide what proportional representation fits — fraction, percentage, or ratio.
  3. Calculate.
  4. Interpret the answer in context.

5Worked example — school attendance

420 students are enrolled. 378 attended Monday. What percentage attended?

1
Decide on the representation
We want one number we can compare across schools and weeks — so percentage is the natural choice.
attended out of total enrolled → use a percentage
2
Calculate
Write the share as a fraction, then convert to a percentage.
378420 = 910 = 0.9 = 90%
3
Interpret in context
A bare "90%" isn't a model yet — we need to say what it means for the school.
90% of enrolled students attended Monday. The school's target is 85%, so Monday is above target.
Answer 90% attended — above the school's 85% target.

6Together — class vote on lunch options

In a class vote: 12 chose pizza, 8 chose sandwiches, 5 chose salad. Express this as a ratio in simplest form. Three small steps — discuss with the person next to you before we fill each box.

💡 Why a ratio here? The interesting question is "how do the three lunch options compare to each other", not "what fraction of the whole class chose each". Part-to-part comparison → ratio.

7Modelling in context — land use on Country 🌾 Applied modelling

The South Australian grain belt is the traditional Country of Ngadjuri, Kaurna and Peramangk peoples, who managed this land for thousands of years before European colonisation. Today the same land is used very differently. Let's model that difference using proportional reasoning.

Imagine a 200-hectare section of the grain belt. We'll use illustrative figures (simplified for a clean comparison) to model two ways of using this same patch of land.

🚜 Modern farm use

  • Wheat: 80 ha
  • Canola: 70 ha
  • Sheep grazing: 50 ha

Total: 200 ha

Illustrative figures for modelling — actual proportions vary across districts.

🌿 Traditional land use

  • Native grasslands (managed with cultural burns): 80 ha
  • Food plants — yam daisy, native millet: 60 ha
  • Wetlands and waterways: 40 ha
  • Stone tool and ceremonial sites: 20 ha

Total: 200 ha

Illustrative figures based on documented First Nations land management practices.

Worked together — express each modern land use as both a fraction and a percentage:

Wheat: 80200 = 25 = 40%  ·  Canola: 70200 = 720 = 35%  ·  Grazing: 50200 = 14 = 25%

And the traditional uses:

Grasslands: 80200 = 40%  ·  Food plants: 60200 = 30%  ·  Wetlands: 40200 = 20%  ·  Ceremony: 20200 = 10%
💬 Discuss with your neighbour: The modern farm has 3 land uses; traditional management had at least 4. What does the proportional comparison tell you about how diversely the land was being managed? (You'll get to write your own modelling answer in the Mastery section.)
