1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's maths.

You've been saving for a new phone. Your budget plan said $480. You get to the shop and the screen tag says $479.95. Close enough?

At the supermarket, you've got $47.85 of groceries in your trolley and a $50 note. Can you afford one more $3 chocolate bar without doing the maths exactly?

And when a calculator shows that 198 × 47 = 930.6 — should you trust it?

Today's idea: rounding turns awkward numbers into friendly ones, and an estimate made from rounded numbers is the quickest way to spot when an answer is wildly off. We'll learn how much precision the situation calls for — sometimes the exact cent, sometimes just "in the right ballpark" — and how to use estimation as a reasonableness check before trusting any calculation.

Round
Tap to flip
Adjust a number to a chosen place value. If the next digit is 5 or more, round up; otherwise round down.
3.847 rounded to 1 d.p. is 3.8.
Decimal place
Tap to flip
A position to the right of the decimal point. 1 d.p. is tenths; 2 d.p. is hundredths; 3 d.p. is thousandths.
In 3.847: the digit at 2 d.p. is 4.
Estimate
Tap to flip
A quick approximate answer found mentally by rounding to friendly numbers.
47 × 198 ≈ 50 × 200 = 10,000.
Magnitude
Tap to flip
The size or scale of a number — is it in the tens, hundreds, thousands?
935 and 9,350 are off by an order of magnitude (a factor of 10).
Reasonable
Tap to flip
Close enough to the estimate to be sensible. An answer off by 10× or more is not reasonable — recheck it.
9,306 is reasonable for 47 × 198 (estimate ≈ 10,000).

5Worked example — estimating the till

Your basket has four items: $3.85, $11.20, $7.99, $0.95. The till's screen shows $24.99. Is that a reasonable total?

1
Round each price to the nearest dollar
Look at the cents. If they're 50¢ or more, round up; otherwise round down.
$3.85→ $4  (85¢ ≥ 50¢)
$11.20→ $11  (20¢ < 50¢)
$7.99→ $8  (99¢ ≥ 50¢)
$0.95→ $1  (95¢ ≥ 50¢)
2
Add the rounded prices
Friendly whole dollars are quick to add in your head.
$4 + $11 + $8 + $1 = $24
3
Compare to the actual
The till shows $24.99. Our estimate is $24. The difference is about $1 — well within the small rounding errors that built up across the four items.
Estimate: $24  |  Till: $24.99  |  Difference: ~ $1  ✓
Conclusion The till's $24.99 is reasonable — pay with confidence.

6Together — same number, three rounded forms

A community pool's renovation cost $4,873.20. Different audiences need different precision. We'll round it three ways — discuss each with your neighbour before we fill it in.

💡 Why this matters: the receipt needs the exact dollar, a spreadsheet looks tidier with the nearest hundred, and a board meeting only needs the nearest thousand. The right amount of precision depends on the audience. Choosing too much detail can be just as unhelpful as too little.
