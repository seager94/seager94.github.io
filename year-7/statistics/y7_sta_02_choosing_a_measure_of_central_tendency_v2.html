Last lesson you learned to calculate the mean, median, mode and range. Today you decide which one to trust. First, three quick recalls.

1Why this matters

A real situation where the "average" can quietly mislead you.

A small online business has 7 people on its payroll. Their weekly pay (in dollars) is:

800, 850, 900, 950, 1000, 1100, 5600

An advert claims: "Join us — our average weekly wage is $1600!" That number is the mean. But six of the seven staff earn $1100 or less. Only the owner, on $5600, earns above $1600.

One unusually large value — an outlier — has dragged the mean far above what most people actually earn. A different summary, the median ($950), sits right among the real wages. Today you learn how to choose the measure of central tendency that tells the honest story about a data set.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. The mean, median and mode are the three measures of central tendency; the range describes spread.

Mode
Tap to flip
The value that appears most often. A data set can have one mode, several, or none.
In 3, 5, 5, 5, 8 the mode is 5.
Median
Tap to flip
The middle value once the data is in order. With an even count, average the middle two.
In 4, 7, 9 the median is 7.
Mean
Tap to flip
Add every value, then divide by how many there are. Often just called the "average".
Mean of 2, 4, 9 = 15 ÷ 3 = 5.
Range
Tap to flip
Highest value minus lowest value. It measures spread, not the centre.
In 6, 9, 14 the range = 14 − 6 = 8.
Outlier
Tap to flip
A value that sits far from the rest of the data. Outliers pull the mean towards them.
In 5, 6, 7, 8, 90 the outlier is 90.

Mean works well here

Use the median instead

💡 The mean is reliable when values cluster together. When a data set has an outlier — an unusually high or low value — it drags the mean away from the centre, so the median becomes the safer summary.

5Worked example 1 — one high earner skews the mean

A small business pays its 7 staff each week (in $): 800, 850, 900, 950, 1000, 1100, 5600. The $5600 belongs to the owner. Which measure best describes a typical wage?

1
Order the data and look for an outlier
Put the wages smallest to largest, then scan for any value far from the rest.
800, 850, 900, 950, 1000, 1100, 5600
The owner's $5600 sits far above the other six wages — it is an outlier.
2
Calculate the mean and the median
Mean = total ÷ how many. Median = the middle value of the ordered list of 7.
Mean = 11200 ÷ 7 = $1600
Median = 4th of the 7 ordered values = $950
3
Decide which measure is fairer — and justify it
Compare each summary against the wages staff actually receive.
Six of the seven staff earn $1100 or less, yet the mean is $1600 — higher than everyone except the owner. The outlier has dragged the mean upward.
The median, $950, lies right among the real wages.
Best summary Median = $950 — the fairer "typical" wage

6Worked example 2 — continuous data from a science experiment

A science class measured the height (in cm) of 8 bean plants after 3 weeks: 18.5, 19.0, 19.5, 20.0, 20.5, 21.0, 21.5, 4.0. Height is continuous numerical data. One plant was knocked over by accident.

1
Identify the anomaly
In an experiment, an anomaly is a reading that does not fit the pattern — often a measurement error.
Ordered: 4.0, 18.5, 19.0, 19.5, 20.0, 20.5, 21.0, 21.5 (cm)
Seven plants cluster between 18.5 and 21.5 cm. The knocked-over plant at 4.0 cm is the anomaly.
2
Compare the measures, with and without the anomaly
Calculate the mean both ways, and the median of all 8 readings.
Mean of all 8 = 144 ÷ 8 = 18.0 cm
Mean of the 7 valid plants = 140 ÷ 7 = 20.0 cm
Median of all 8 = (19.5 + 20.0) ÷ 2 = 19.75 cm
3
Decide and justify
Which value honestly describes the typical growth of a healthy plant?
The single anomaly pulled the mean down by 2 cm (20.0 → 18.0 cm). The median moved hardly at all.
Report the median — or the mean of the valid readings — and record the anomaly separately so the experiment stays honest.
Best summary Median = 19.75 cm — barely shifted by the anomaly

7Worked example 3 — reading all four measures from a data display

A frequency table shows how many books 10 students read over the summer holidays. Calculate the mean, median, mode and range, then decide which describes a typical student.

Books read234519
Students23221
1
Read the display and list the values
Each "Books read" value is repeated as many times as the frequency says.
Listed out: 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 19
That is 10 values. The student who read 19 books stands far from the rest — an outlier.
2
Calculate all four measures
Mean, median and mode describe the centre; range describes the spread.
Mean = 50 ÷ 10 = 5.0 books
Median = (5th + 6th) ÷ 2 = (3 + 4) ÷ 2 = 3.5 books
Mode = 3 books (read by 3 students)
Range = 19 − 2 = 17 books
3
Explain how the outlier changes the picture
Compare each measure with what the students actually did.
Nine of the ten students read 5 books or fewer, yet the mean is 5.0 — inflated by the one student who read 19. The median (3.5) and mode (3) describe a typical student far better. The large range (17) is itself driven by that outlier.
Best summary Median 3.5 and mode 3 — not the outlier-inflated mean of 5.0
