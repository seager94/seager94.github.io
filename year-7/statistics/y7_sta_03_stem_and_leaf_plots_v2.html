1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's statistics.

Mr Tran measured the height of every student in class 7C and scribbled 24 numbers down a page in no particular order:

145  132  168  124  151  137  142  156  131  149  128  163  135  172  145  …

Staring at that list, he can't quickly see the shortest student, the tallest, or the typical height. He needs the numbers organised — without losing a single original value.

A stem-and-leaf plot solves exactly this. It splits each height into a stem (the first digits) and a leaf (the last digit), then stacks them in order. The shortest, the tallest, the clusters and the gaps all jump out — and every original number is still there to read back.

Example 1 — Constructing an ordered stem-and-leaf plot

Twelve Year 7 students had their heights measured (cm): 145, 132, 158, 141, 137, 152, 149, 133, 145, 144, 138, 150. Construct an ordered stem-and-leaf plot.

1
Choose the stems
The smallest value is 132 cm and the largest is 158 cm. The stem is the hundreds-and-tens part, so we need stems 13, 14, 15.
Smallest = 132 → stem 13    Largest = 158 → stem 15
2
Place each leaf with its stem
Work through the list in order. The ones digit becomes the leaf. This first plot is unordered — the leaves are just in the order they were recorded.
StemLeaf (unordered)
132 7 3 8
145 1 9 5 4
158 2 0
3
Order the leaves and add a key
Sort each row's leaves smallest to largest. A key tells the reader how to decode a stem and a leaf.
StemLeaf (ordered)
132 3 7 8
141 4 5 5 9
150 2 8
 Key: 13 | 2 = 132 cm
Answer An ordered stem-and-leaf plot — all 12 values kept, now sorted and grouped.
Example 2 — Reading centre and spread from a plot

Using the ordered plot from Example 1, find the range, the median and the mode of the 12 heights.

StemLeaf
132 3 7 8
141 4 5 5 9
150 2 8
 Key: 13 | 2 = 132 cm
1
Find the spread — the range
A plot's first and last leaves are its smallest and largest values. Range = largest − smallest.
Range = 158 − 132 = 26 cm
2
Locate the median position
There are n = 12 leaves (even), so the median sits halfway between the 6th and 7th values. Count leaves from the top row down.
132, 133, 137, 138, 141, 144, 145, 145, 149, 150, 152, 158
6th value = 144,   7th value = 145
3
Calculate median and read the mode
Average the two middle values for the median. The mode is the most frequent value — easy to spot as a repeated leaf.
Median = (144 + 145) ÷ 2 = 144.5 cm
Mode = 145 cm (leaf 5 appears twice in the stem-14 row)
Answer Range 26 cm  ·  Median 144.5 cm  ·  Mode 145 cm
Example 3 — Comparing two groups with a back-to-back plot

The heights of a netball squad and a basketball squad were recorded. Compare the two groups using this back-to-back stem-and-leaf plot.

Netball leafStemBasketball leaf
8 213
9 6 3 1141 5 8
2152 5 8
161 7
 Key: 2 | 13 = 132 cm (netball)  ·  14 | 1 = 141 cm (basketball)
1
Compare the centre
Netball has 7 values: median is the 4th = 143 cm. Basketball has 8 values: median is between the 4th and 5th = (152 + 155) ÷ 2 = 153.5 cm.
Netball median = 143 cm  <  Basketball median = 153.5 cm
The basketball squad is taller on average.
2
Compare the spread
Range = largest − smallest for each squad.
Netball range = 152 − 132 = 20 cm
Basketball range = 167 − 141 = 26 cm — basketball heights are more spread out.
3
Describe the shape
Read the lengths of the rows on each side.
Netball clusters tightly in the 140s and is roughly symmetric.
Basketball stretches upward into the 160s — it is skewed towards the taller values.
Answer Basketball is taller (higher centre), more spread out, and skewed high; netball is shorter and roughly symmetric.
