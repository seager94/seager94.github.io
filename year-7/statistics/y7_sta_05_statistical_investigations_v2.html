1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's statistics.

A school STEM club builds paper gliders. The team thinks that adding winglets — small upturned wing-tips — will make a glider fly further.

One member throws the modified glider once, it sails a long way, and they announce: "It works — I saw it!" A teammate is not convinced. Is one lucky throw really evidence?

To answer a question fairly with data, you run a statistical investigation: pose a clear question, plan how to collect data, gather a sample, summarise it with statistics, then report what the numbers actually show. One throw is an anecdote. A well-planned investigation is evidence — and that is what today's lesson builds.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. You'll use these five words constantly today.

Variable
Tap to flip
A quantity that is measured or counted and varies between individuals.
Reaction time, height and number of goals are all variables.
Discrete data
Tap to flip
Numerical data that takes only separate, countable values — usually whole numbers.
Number of paper planes thrown: 0, 1, 2, 3 … never 2.7.
Continuous data
Tap to flip
Numerical data that can take any value in a range — usually from measuring.
A reaction time of 0.213 s, or a flight of 6.27 m.
Distribution
Tap to flip
The pattern of how the data is spread — where it clusters, how wide it is, its shape.
Most teen reaction times cluster near 210 ms.
Summary statistic
Tap to flip
A single number describing one feature of a data set.
Mean, median and mode describe the centre; range describes the spread.

5Worked examples — the investigation cycle

Every statistical investigation follows the same five stages. The three examples below walk one full cycle for the reaction-time investigation.

1 · Pose a question→ 2 · Plan→ 3 · Collect data→ 4 · Analyse→ 5 · Interpret & report

Example 1 — Pose the question & plan

Investigation: Do teenagers have faster reaction times than adults? (AC9M7ST03, elaboration 1)

1
Pose a clear, testable question
A good investigation question is specific and can be answered by collecting data.
"Do teenagers have faster reaction times than adults?"
✓ Specific (two named groups)   ✓ Comparable   ✓ Answerable with measured data
2
Identify and classify the variable
Name what you will measure, then decide whether it is discrete or continuous.
Variable measured: reaction time → continuous numerical data (it is measured and can take any value in a range).
Grouping variable: age group (teenager / adult) → categorical — it sorts people into two groups to compare.
3
Plan a fair method
Decide the sample and how to keep the test fair, so the comparison is trustworthy.
  • Sample: 10 teenagers and 10 adults.
  • Same test for everyone: the same reaction-time test (e.g. a ruler-drop test).
  • Same conditions: quiet room, same number of attempts each.
  • Record: each time in milliseconds (ms), then compare summary statistics.
Stages 1 & 2 complete A clear question + a fair plan = a trustworthy investigation.

Example 2 — Collect & analyse the data

The teenage sample was collected: 210, 195, 230, 205, 240, 220, 200, 215, 225, 200 (ms).

1
Organise the data
Sort the values from lowest to highest — this makes the median and range easy to read.
195, 200, 200, 205, 210, 215, 220, 225, 230, 240
2
Calculate the mean and the median
The mean adds every value and shares it out; the median is the middle value.
Mean = (210+195+230+205+240+220+200+215+225+200) ÷ 10
= 2140 ÷ 10 = 214 ms

Median: 10 values, so average the middle two (5th and 6th):
= (210 + 215) ÷ 2 = 212.5 ms
3
Find the mode and the range
The mode is the most common value; the range measures the spread.
Mode = 200 ms (the only value that appears twice)
Range = 240 − 195 = 45 ms
Teenage sample — analysed Mean 214 ms · Median 212.5 ms · Mode 200 ms · Range 45 ms

Example 3 — Interpret & report the findings

The adult sample was analysed the same way: mean 265 ms, median 262.5 ms, mode 260 ms, range 45 ms.

1
Compare the summary statistics
Put the two distributions side by side and look at centre and spread.
StatisticTeenagersAdults
Mean214 ms265 ms
Median212.5 ms262.5 ms
Range45 ms45 ms
Centre: teenagers are about 51 ms lower (265 − 214). Spread: identical (range 45 ms each).
2
Describe the shape of each distribution
Report findings in terms of shape, not just numbers.
Each sample has a single peak and is fairly evenly spread around its centre, with no extreme outliers. The two distributions have the same shape and width — the adult distribution is simply shifted about 51 ms higher.
3
Write the conclusion — answer the original question
A report links the evidence straight back to the question, and notes any limitations.
"The teenage sample had a lower mean (214 ms) and median (212.5 ms) reaction time than the adult sample (265 ms and 262.5 ms), by about 51 ms, with the same spread. This supports the claim that teenagers react faster than adults. Because each sample is only 10 people, testing larger samples would make the conclusion more reliable."
Investigation cycle complete The data supports the claim — teenagers reacted faster.
