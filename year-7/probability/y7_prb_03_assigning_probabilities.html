1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's probability.

In the board game Get Home First, you can only move a token out of the start zone by rolling a 6. It can feel like a 6 never turns up — yet across one full game you might roll the dice around 60 times.

So how many 6s should you really expect in a game? Guessing isn't good enough. There's a way to predict it.

Every roll, the chance of a 6 is exactly the same: 16. Over about 60 rolls, you can predict that roughly one-sixth of them are sixes — that's 60 ÷ 6 = 10 sixes. Today you'll learn to assign a probability to an outcome and use it to predict how often that outcome happens.

5Worked examples

Three examples. First we assign a probability; then we use it to predict how often an outcome happens. Use the button to reveal each step.

Example 1 — Assign a probability to a single outcome

A standard dice is rolled once. What is the probability of throwing a 6?

1
Identify the sample space
List every possible outcome of this single-stage event.
Sample space: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 — six equally likely outcomes.
2
Assign the probability
Count the favourable outcomes, then write the fraction: favourable ÷ total.
P(6) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes
P(6) = 1 ÷ 6 = 16
3
Check it makes sense
A probability must sit between 0 and 1.
16 is between 0 and 1 — a valid probability. A 6 is unlikely on any single roll.
Answer P(6) = 16

Example 2 — Predict a frequency over many trials

A dice is rolled 60 times. Predict how many times it will show a 6.

1
Recall the probability
We assigned this in Example 1.
P(6) = 16
2
Multiply by the number of trials
Expected frequency = probability × number of rolls.
Expected 6s = 16 × 60
= 60 ÷ 6 = 10
3
Interpret the prediction
A prediction is the most likely result — not a guarantee.
We expect about 10 sixes. The real result may be 8, 11 or 13 — but 10 is the best single prediction.
Answer ≈ 10 sixes in 60 rolls

Example 3 — Assign, then predict, with larger numbers

A dice is rolled 300 times. Predict how many results are a 6, and how many are not a 6.

1
Assign both probabilities
One face is a 6; the other five faces are "not a 6".
P(6) = 16  ·  P(not a 6) = 56
2
Predict each frequency
Multiply each probability by 300 trials.
Sixes: 16 × 300 = 300 ÷ 6 = 50
Not sixes: 56 × 300 = 5 × 50 = 250
3
Check the predictions
Every roll is either a 6 or not a 6 — the two predictions should total 300.
50 + 250 = 300 ✓ — every roll is accounted for.
Answer 50 sixes  ·  250 non-sixes
