1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's probability.

Your friend tosses a coin 6 times. It lands on Heads 5 times.

"This coin is lucky!" she says — and refuses to swap it for the start of a game.

Is the coin really unfair... or is 6 tosses simply too few to tell?

With only a few trials, results jump around — 5 Heads from 6 tosses happens easily with a perfectly fair coin. But run a coin or dice experiment hundreds or thousands of times — using a digital simulator — and the relative frequency settles down close to the theoretical probability. Today you'll see that settling for yourself. It's called the law of large numbers.

6I Do — comparing predicted and observed results

Three examples, all the same routine: predict using theoretical probability, record the observed result, then compare and explain the difference. Use the reveal buttons to step through each one.

Example 1 — a coin tossed 40 times

A fair coin is tossed 40 times. Predict how many Heads to expect, then compare with an experiment that produced 24 Heads.

1
Predict using theoretical probability
A fair coin has 2 equally likely outcomes, so P(Heads) = 12. Expected count = probability × number of trials.
Predicted Heads = 12 × 40 = 20
2
Record the observed result
The experiment actually gave 24 Heads. The relative frequency is the observed count over the number of trials.
Relative frequency of Heads = 2440 = 0.60
3
Compare and explain the difference
Predicted 0.50, observed 0.60 — a gap of 0.10. With only 40 trials, random variation easily pushes the relative frequency away from the theoretical probability. This does not mean the coin is biased.
Conclusion A 0.10 gap is normal for just 40 trials — too few to judge the coin.

Example 2 — a die rolled 60 times

A fair six-sided die is rolled 60 times. Predict the number of 5s, then compare with an experiment that produced 13 fives.

1
Predict using theoretical probability
A fair die has 6 equally likely outcomes, so P(5) = 16.
Predicted fives = 16 × 60 = 10
2
Record the observed result
The experiment gave 13 fives. Write the relative frequency as a decimal.
Relative frequency of 5 = 1360 ≈ 0.217
3
Compare and explain the difference
Theoretical probability ≈ 0.167, observed ≈ 0.217 — a gap of about 0.05. 60 trials is still a small sample, so a gap like this is expected. We would predict that rolling the die many more times brings the relative frequency back toward 16.
Conclusion Observed 0.217 vs predicted 0.167 — a small-sample gap, not a faulty die.

Example 3 — a spinner simulated 2000 times

A digital tool simulates a 4-colour spinner (equal sections) 2000 times. Predict the number landing on Blue, then compare with the simulation's result of 486 Blue.

1
Predict using theoretical probability
The spinner has 4 equal sections, so P(Blue) = 14.
Predicted Blue = 14 × 2000 = 500
2
Record the observed result
The simulation recorded 486 Blue out of 2000 trials.
Relative frequency of Blue = 4862000 = 0.243
3
Compare and explain the difference
Theoretical probability 0.250, observed 0.243 — a gap of only 0.007. With a large number of trials the relative frequency is very close to the theoretical probability. Compare the three examples: 40 trials gave a 0.10 gap, 60 trials gave a 0.05 gap, but 2000 trials gave just 0.007. That shrinking gap is the law of large numbers.
Conclusion More trials → smaller gap. 2000 trials lands within 0.007 of the prediction.
