1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's maths.

Open a weather app and you'll see something like "70% chance of rain today." A footy commentator says a team is "unlikely" to come back. A friend says it's "a coin toss" who'll win.

All of these are statements about chance — how likely something is to happen. People use these words loosely, but mathematicians use them precisely.

Today we learn the exact language of probability. Every event can be described with one of five words — impossible, unlikely, even chance, likely or certain — and given a number from 0 to 1 on a probability scale.
0Impossible 0.25Unlikely 0.5Even chance 0.75Likely 1Certain
💡 The further right an event sits, the more likely it is. Nothing can ever sit below 0 or above 1.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. These six words are the language of chance — you'll use them constantly today.

Experiment
Tap to flip
A repeatable activity with a result that is not known in advance.
Tossing a coin or rolling a die is an experiment.
Trial
Tap to flip
One single performance of the experiment.
One toss of the coin is one trial. Tossing it 20 times is 20 trials.
Event
Tap to flip
A result, or group of results, that you are interested in.
"Rolling an even number" is an event.
Favourable outcome
Tap to flip
An outcome that counts as the event happening.
For "roll an even number", the favourable outcomes are 2, 4 and 6.
Sample space
Tap to flip
The complete list of every possible outcome of the experiment.
Tossing a coin: the sample space is {heads, tails}.
Probability
Tap to flip
A measure of how likely an event is, on a scale from 0 to 1.
The probability of a coin landing heads is 1/2.

5Worked examples — classify & place on the scale

For each event I follow the same 3-step routine: name the experiment and its outcomes → choose the chance word → place it on the 0–1 scale. Use the "Reveal next step" button to follow along.

Example 1 — "The sun will set this evening."

1
Name the experiment and its outcomes
The experiment is observing whether the sun sets today. There are only two possible outcomes.
Sample space: { the sun sets, the sun does not set }
2
Choose the chance word
Every day in recorded history, the sun has set. There is no way for it not to — the event is guaranteed.
The sun will set → Certain
3
Place it on the 0–1 scale
A certain event always happens, so it sits at the far right of the scale — a probability of 1.
Sun sets
0Impossible 0.25Unlikely 0.5Even chance 0.75Likely 1Certain
Answer Certain — probability = 1

Example 2 — "A tossed coin lands on tails."

1
Name the experiment and its outcomes
The experiment is tossing a fair coin once — that single toss is one trial. There are two equally likely outcomes.
Sample space: { heads, tails } — 2 outcomes
2
Choose the chance word
One outcome (tails) is favourable. Both outcomes are equally likely, so the event has the same chance of happening as not happening.
1 of 2 equally likely outcomes → Even chance
3
Place it on the 0–1 scale
An even-chance event sits exactly halfway along the scale, at a probability of 12 = 0.5.
Coin = tails
0Impossible 0.25Unlikely 0.5Even chance 0.75Likely 1Certain
Answer Even chance — probability = 12

Example 3 — "Rolling a 5 on a standard six-sided die."

1
Name the experiment and its outcomes
The experiment is rolling a die once. The sample space is the six numbers on the die, and only one of them is favourable for our event.
Sample space: { 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 } — favourable: { 5 }
2
Choose the chance word
Only 1 of the 6 outcomes is favourable — that is a small share, well below half — but it can still happen.
1 of 6 outcomes → Unlikely
3
Place it on the 0–1 scale
The probability is 16 ≈ 0.17 — near the low end of the scale, but not all the way down at 0.
Roll a 5
0Impossible 0.25Unlikely 0.5Even chance 0.75Likely 1Certain
Answer Unlikely — probability = 16
