1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's geometry.

Mia runs a paving business. Her stone supplier's catalogue lists pavers only by precise shape name — square, rectangle, rhombus, parallelogram, kite, trapezium.

Last week an apprentice phoned in an order for "300 four-sided pavers". The truck arrived with a random mix — some rhombuses, some trapeziums — and not one of them fitted the rectangular courtyard border Mia had planned. The whole pallet had to go back.

"Quadrilateral" only means four sides — it is not precise enough to order, build or describe a shape. Today you'll classify quadrilaterals exactly by their side and angle properties, and see how the families overlap: why every square is also a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square.

5Worked example 1 — classify by properties

PQRS has both pairs of opposite sides parallel and equal in length. Its angles are 70°, 110°, 70° and 110° — none are right angles, and its adjacent sides are different lengths. Classify it.

1
Identify the properties
List what we are told about sides and angles.
parallel sides: 2 pairs  ·  opposite sides equal: ✓  ·  all four sides equal: ✗  ·  right angles: ✗
2
Match to a definition
Use each property to rule families out.
Two pairs of parallel sides  →  rules out kite and trapezium
No right angles  →  rules out rectangle and square
Sides not all equal  →  rules out rhombus
3
Verify and relate
Check the survivor against its definition, then place it in the family tree.
A parallelogram needs only two pairs of parallel sides — PQRS has them. It is not a rectangle (no right angles) or a rhombus (sides not all equal).
Answer PQRS is a parallelogram

6Worked example 2 — contrast with a parallelogram

WXYZ has exactly one pair of parallel sides. The other two sides are not parallel and not equal. Classify it.

1
Identify the properties
The deciding property here is how many pairs of parallel sides there are.
parallel sides: exactly 1 pair  ·  opposite sides equal: ✗  ·  right angles: ✗
2
Match to a definition
Compare the count of parallel pairs against each family.
parallelogram, rectangle, rhombus, square  →  all need two pairs
kite  →  needs zero pairs
exactly one pair  →  trapezium
3
Verify and contrast
Hold the trapezium up against the parallelogram from Example 1.
A parallelogram has two pairs of parallel sides; a trapezium has exactly one. So a trapezium is never a parallelogram — one pair is not enough.
Answer WXYZ is a trapezium

7Worked example 3 — relationships between families

A square ABCD has four equal sides and four right angles. Which other quadrilateral names also describe it correctly?

1
Identify the square's properties
Write down everything a square is guaranteed to have.
four equal sides: ✓  ·  four right angles: ✓  ·  two pairs of parallel sides: ✓
2
Test each family's definition
Ask: does the square meet the entry requirement for each family?
rectangle needs four right angles  →  ✓
rhombus needs four equal sides  →  ✓
parallelogram needs two pairs of parallel sides  →  ✓
3
Verify and relate
State the relationship — and check it does not work in reverse.
A square sits inside the rectangle, rhombus and parallelogram families. But the reverse fails: a 6 cm × 3 cm rectangle has no four equal sides, so it is not a square.
Answer A square is also a rectangle, a rhombus and a parallelogram
🔑 The big idea: the families are nested. Every square is a rectangle and a rhombus; every rectangle and every rhombus is a parallelogram. The most precise name is the one with the most properties — always give that one.
