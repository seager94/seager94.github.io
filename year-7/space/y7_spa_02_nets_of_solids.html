1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's geometry.

Have you noticed that cardboard boxes arrive at a shop folded completely flat? A box leaves the factory as a flat sheet of cardboard and is only folded into its 3D shape when it's needed.

The flat sheet is printed, measured and cut while it lies flat on a machine — far easier than working on a finished 3D box. But the designer has to be sure the flat sheet will actually fold up into the right solid before printing thousands of them.

That flat sheet is a net — a 2D pattern that folds into a 3D solid. Today you'll learn to look at a net and work out which solid it folds into: a cube, a rectangular prism, a triangular prism, or a pyramid. You'll also see why a net that looks right can sometimes fail to fold correctly.

Example 1 — Does Net A fold to a cube?

Net A is shown below.

Net A
1
Count the faces
A cube has 6 faces and every one is a square.
Net A has 6 square faces. The face count matches a cube — so far, so good.
2
Imagine folding it
Counting is not enough — check that the arrangement folds correctly.
Fold the vertical strip of four squares into a loop: they become the four side faces. The left and right squares fold up to cap the top and bottom. No two squares land in the same place.
3
Decide
Six square faces, in an arrangement that folds with no overlaps and no gaps.
Every face lands in a different place when folded.
Answer Yes — Net A folds into a cube.

Example 2 — Which solid does Net B fold into?

Net B is shown below.

Net B
1
Identify and count the faces
Name every face in the net before deciding anything.
3 rectangles + 2 triangles = 5 faces.
2
Match the faces to a solid
Think about which faces become the ends and which become the sides.
The two identical triangles become the ends; the three rectangles wrap around as the sides. A solid with two triangular ends joined by rectangles is a triangular prism.
3
Verify by folding
Fold the triangles up and wrap the rectangles around them.
The two triangles become the parallel ends; the rectangles become the three sides.
Answer Net B folds into a triangular prism.

Example 3 — Does Net C fold to a cube?

Net C is shown below.

Net C
1
Count the faces
Start the same way — count the squares.
Net C has 6 square faces. The count matches a cube, so this net looks fine.
2
Test the fold
Now actually fold it — this is where counting is not enough.
Folding this 2-by-3 block, two squares fold on top of each other and one face of the cube is left uncovered. The faces overlap.
3
Decide — and reason about the representation
A correct face count does not guarantee a working net.
Net C does not fold into a cube. This is a disadvantage of a net: an arrangement can look reasonable yet fail to fold — you must mentally fold it to be sure.
Answer No — Net C does not fold into a cube.
