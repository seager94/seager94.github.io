1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's geometry.

When you buy a flat-pack desk, the instruction booklet has to show a 3D object — the finished desk — on a flat 2D page. The designers can't hand you the desk; they hand you a picture of it.

The same problem faces architects, engineers, video-game artists and anyone who builds from a plan: how do you squash three dimensions onto two?

A 3D solid on a 2D page
There is more than one way to flatten 3D into 2D. An isometric drawing keeps every edge true to scale, so you can measure it. A set of orthographic views — front, side and top — shows each face exactly. A perspective drawing looks the most realistic. Today you'll learn to read and draw all three, and to reason about when each one is the best choice.

Views that share a measurement must match. The front and top views both show a width of 3. The front and side views both show a height of 2. They agree — so the views are correct.

6Worked example 2 — interpreting an isometric drawing

This isometric drawing looks like a solid block. We'll count how many cubes built it — including the ones we cannot see.

A solid block (isometric)
1
Count the cubes you can see
The block has two layers, each looking like a 2 × 2 square. From this corner view you can trace 7 separate cubes across the two layers.
Visible cubes counted from the drawing: 7
2
Find the hidden cube
An isometric drawing can hide cubes. The cube at the back-bottom-inner corner is completely out of sight — but it must be there to support the cube resting on top of it.
Hidden cubes you must include: 1
3
Add them together
Total = visible + hidden = 7 + 1 = 8. We can check this: the block is 2 wide × 2 deep × 2 high, and 2 × 2 × 2 = 8.
7 visible + 1 hidden = 8 cubes
Answer 8 cubes — and 1 of them is completely hidden

7Worked example 3 — isometric vs perspective

The same box is drawn two ways. We'll reason about the advantages and disadvantages of each, and decide who should use which.

Isometric drawing
Perspective drawing
1
Read the isometric drawing
Parallel edges of the box stay parallel on the page, and every edge is drawn to the same scale. You can measure a length directly by counting the cube units along an edge.
Advantage: measurable. Disadvantage: the box looks slightly unnatural — your eye expects far edges to shrink.
2
Read the perspective drawing
Edges that are parallel in real life converge to a vanishing point, so the back of the box is drawn smaller than the front. It looks the way the box really appears to your eye.
Advantage: realistic — looks like a photograph. Disadvantage: not to scale, so you cannot measure the box from the drawing.
3
Decide who uses which
An engineer or builder needs exact measurements, so they choose the isometric drawing. An advertiser or artist wants the box to look real and appealing, so they choose the perspective drawing. The best representation depends on the job it has to do.
Engineer → isometric (measurable) · Advertiser → perspective (realistic)
Big idea Isometric = measurable but less lifelike · Perspective = lifelike but not measurable
