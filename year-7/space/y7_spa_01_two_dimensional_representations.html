1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's geometry.

A box of cereal sits on a shelf — a solid 3D object. But before it was ever filled, it started life as a single flat sheet of cardboard, printed and cut, then folded into a box.

Architects work the other way around. Before a building exists, they draw it flat — a plan view looking straight down, showing exactly where every wall and room will sit.

In both cases a 3D object is captured on a flat, 2D page. The trick is choosing a flat representation that shows what you actually need.

Today we represent solid objects in two dimensions in two ways: by deconstructing packaging into a flat net, and by drawing a building's aerial (plan) view. Then we reason about what each representation shows well — and what it leaves out.

5Worked example 1 — deconstruct the packaging

A tissue box is a rectangular prism. Cut along its edges, unfold it flat — what 2D net do you get?

1
Look at the 3D object
A tissue box is a rectangular prism. It has 6 flat faces, and opposite faces are identical pairs: top & bottom, front & back, the two ends.
A rectangular prism — a solid 3D object
2
Unfold it into a net
Imagine cutting along the edges and laying every face flat on the table. Each face becomes a 2D rectangle. The flat pattern is the net.
The net — the box represented flat in two dimensions
3
Check the net
Count the rectangles: 6 in total, forming 3 matching pairs (top/bottom, front/back, two ends). Mentally fold the net back along the joins — it rebuilds the box, so the net is correct.
6 rectangles · 3 matching pairs — fold the net along the edges and the box comes back.
Answer A net of 6 rectangles (3 matching pairs)

6Worked example 2 — draw the aerial view

A single-storey building is shaped like the letter L. Draw its plan view — the footprint seen from directly above.

1
Picture the 3D building
Here is the building drawn from an angle. It has walls, height and a roof — a true 3D object standing on the ground.
An L-shaped building — seen from an angle
2
Move your viewpoint directly above
Imagine a drone hovering straight above the building, looking straight down. Height and walls disappear — you see only the outline of the ground the building covers.
Looking straight down ↓ — the height vanishes, and only the outline of the footprint is left.
3
Trace the footprint
Trace the outline you see from above onto a grid. This flat 2D drawing is the plan view (aerial view). Here it is an L-shape made of 5 unit squares.
The plan view — the building's footprint in two dimensions
Answer Plan view = an L-shape of 5 unit squares

7Worked example 3 — which representation is better?

You want a friend to build the same cardboard box you have. Should you send them a net or a photograph of the finished box? Reason about both.

1
Option A — a photograph of the finished box
Think about what a photo tells your friend, and what it hides.
✓ Advantage: shows what the box looks like assembled — its colour, proportions and how it sits. Easy to recognise at a glance.
✗ Disadvantage: hides the exact size of each face and how the faces fold together. Your friend can't build it from a photo alone.
2
Option B — the net
Now think about what the net tells your friend, and what it hides.
✓ Advantage: shows every face, its exact measurements, and how the faces connect and fold. Your friend can cut, fold and build it.
✗ Disadvantage: harder to picture the finished 3D object — a net laid flat doesn't obviously look like a box.
3
Reason and decide
For the job of building the box, the net is the better representation — it carries the measurements and folding information. For the job of recognising the box on a shelf, the photo wins. The "best" representation depends on the question you are answering.
Net → best for building it. Photo → best for recognising it. Different jobs, different representations.
Big idea Different representations have different strengths — choose the one that shows what your reader needs
