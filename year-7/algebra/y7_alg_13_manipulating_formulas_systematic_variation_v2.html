1Why this matters

One formula, hundreds of answers — if you have the right tool.

Your friend asks: "Pizza Plus charges $15 per pizza plus a $10 delivery fee. How much for the team?"

  • 4 pizzas → ?
  • 7 pizzas → ?
  • 12 pizzas → ?
  • 20 pizzas → ?

You could do four separate calculations. Or you could write the formula C = 15n + 10 and let a table answer every question at once.

Big idea A formula compresses many calculations into one rule. Change one input and the output changes predictably. A reactive table reveals the pattern instantly — you don't have to imagine it, you can see it.

3First reactive table — volume of a box

Volume of a rectangular prism is V = l × w × h. We've fixed the width and built a table of the other two. Drag the w slider — watch every cell update at once.

V = l × w × h
w = 4 Width — drag me
Rows: l = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Columns: h = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Each cell shows V for that combination.
Notice this Look at any row (fix l) and read across as h grows: V increases by the same amount each step. Look at any column and read down: same pattern. Now compare l = 2 with l = 4 in the same column — what do you see? When one input doubles, the output doubles too.

4Same volume, different shapes

A box has volume V = 36 cm³. Many different boxes can have this volume — they just have to multiply to 36. Type values into each row; the row turns green when the volume hits 36.

V = l × w × h · target: 36
Click Show three examples to fill the first three rows with 2 × 3 × 6, 4 × 3 × 3, and 1 × 6 × 6. They look like completely different boxes — but they all hold the same amount.
Key takeaway Many sets of inputs can give the same output. One output doesn't tell you the inputs — but it does tell you a relationship the inputs must satisfy (here, l × w × h = 36).

5Worked example — distance, speed, time

Use the formula d = s × t (distance = speed × time). The bus travels at a constant s = 60 km/h. Build a table of distance for t = 1, 2, 3, 4 hours.

1
Identify the inputs and the output
In this formula, s and t are inputs and d is the output. We've fixed s = 60. Only t will vary.
Inputs: s = 60 (fixed), t (varies: 1, 2, 3, 4)
Output: d
2
Substitute each input — fill the table
For each row, swap t for the number and multiply.
t = 1 → d = 60 × 1 = 60
t = 2 → d = 60 × 2 = 120
t = 3 → d = 60 × 3 = 180
t = 4 → d = 60 × 4 = 240
3
Spot the pattern
Every time t goes up by 1 hour, d goes up by 60 km. When t doubles (1 → 2), d also doubles (60 → 120). We say d is proportional to t.
Now drag the s-slider below — watch every cell of the table change at once. Try s = 80 (a faster bus): every value scales up.
Big idea A table doesn't just answer the questions — it makes the pattern visible.
d = s × t
s = 60 km/h Speed — drag me
Try s = 60, then s = 120. Notice: every cell exactly doubles, because the speed doubled.
