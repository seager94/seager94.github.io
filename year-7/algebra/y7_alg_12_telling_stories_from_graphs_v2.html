1Why this matters

A graph is a compressed story.

Imagine someone hands you a written paragraph describing the temperature in Adelaide every minute for a day. You'd be reading for an hour and you'd still struggle to spot the hottest moment.

Now imagine the same data as a graph. In one glance you can see the cold morning low, the afternoon peak, the cooling at night — and exactly when each happens.

A graph compresses a story into a picture. Today we learn to read three clues:
  • the slope — how fast something changes
  • the peaks and troughs — the highest and lowest moments
  • the flat sections — the resting points where nothing changes

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. You'll use these five words constantly today.

Axis
Tap to flip
A horizontal or vertical reference line. Each axis tells you what is being measured and in what units.
On a temperature graph: the x-axis shows hours, the y-axis shows °C.
Slope
Tap to flip
How steep a line is. A steep line means a fast change; a shallow line means a slow change.
On a distance–time graph, a steeper slope means a faster speed.
Peak
Tap to flip
The highest point on the graph. The story's "biggest moment".
Adelaide on a 38°C day — the peak is at 3pm.
Trough
Tap to flip
The lowest point on the graph. The story's "quietest moment".
On a 24-hour temperature graph, the trough is usually just before sunrise.
Horizontal segment
Tap to flip
A flat section of the graph. The value isn't changing — often a rest, pause or "stuck" moment.
A cyclist's distance stays at 10 km while she rests — flat line.

💡 The big idea: when distance vs time shows a horizontal segment, the cyclist isn't moving. That flat bit of line is hiding a whole story — a rest, a stop, a chat with a friend.

5Worked example — reading a cyclist's travel graph

A cyclist rides for half an hour, rests, then rides on. Here's her distance from home over time.

Cyclist's distance from home (km) vs time (minutes)
1
Identify the three phases
Look at the shape. Find where the line rises, stays flat, and rises again. Each phase is part of the story.
Phase 1 (0–30 min): the line rises from 0 to 10 km — she's riding.
Phase 2 (30–60 min): the line is flat at 10 km — she's resting for 30 minutes.
Phase 3 (60–90 min): the line rises from 10 to 15 km — she's riding again.
2
Average speed in Phase 1
Speed = distance ÷ time. Convert minutes to hours by dividing by 60.
Distance: 10 km. Time: 30 min = 3060 h = 0.5 h
Speed = 100.5 = 20 km/h
3
Average speed in Phase 3 — and what the rest tells us
Same method — distance ÷ time. Then compare to Phase 1.
Phase 3 distance: 15 − 10 = 5 km. Time: 30 min = 0.5 h
Speed = 50.5 = 10 km/h

Story: She was twice as fast before her rest. Maybe she's tired, or maybe the second leg is uphill. Either way, the flat segment tells us a story the numbers alone don't — a 30-minute pause.
The graph's story Rode 10 km at 20 km/h → rested 30 min → rode 5 km at 10 km/h.
