1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's algebra.

Every Saturday at the MCG, the scoreboard operator works out the total points for an AFL team using this rule:

points = 6 × goals + behinds

The operator never works it out from scratch — they just plug today's numbers into the rule. If a team kicks 12 goals and 7 behinds, the rule gives 6 × 12 + 7 = 79 points.

That rule is a formula. We write it with letters: p = 6g + b. The letters stand for changing quantities (goals, behinds, points). To use a formula, we substitute — swap each letter for its actual number — and then evaluate. Today you'll learn to do this for area, perimeter, distance, heart rate, wages and more.

5Worked example — the 3-step routine

Problem: A rectangle has length l = 8 m and width w = 5 m. Use the formula A = l × w to find the area.

1
Read the formula and identify what each letter means
The formula A = l × w is a rule. It says: to find the area of any rectangle, multiply its length by its width. So A stands for area, l stands for length, and w stands for width. We are given the values for l and w — we have to find the value for A.
A = l × w    where l = 8 m and w = 5 m
2
Substitute — swap each letter for its number
Replace l with 8 and w with 5. Don't drop the units and don't drop the multiplication sign. Some teachers put the numbers in brackets — this is a tidy habit that prevents mistakes later when values are negative.
A = (8) × (5)
3
Evaluate — work out the answer and add the units
Now it's just arithmetic. 8 × 5 = 40. Because we multiplied metres by metres, the area is in square metres (m²). Always finish with units — a number alone isn't a real-world answer.
A = 8 × 5
A = 40 m²
Answer A = 40 m²
💡 The 3-step routine for every formula: (1) read the formula and name each variable, (2) substitute the given values, (3) evaluate and label the units. Stick to this routine and you'll be safe on any formula — even ones you've never seen before.

💡 Why "order of operations" matters here: the formula is 6g + b. The 6 is multiplying g, so we do that multiplication first, before the addition.
