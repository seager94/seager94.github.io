1Why this matters

Mathematicians compress sentences into expressions — and the rules are strict.

Imagine your phone plan: "It costs $15 a month, plus $2 for every gigabyte of data you use." That's a full English sentence — twelve words and a comma.

A mathematician compresses it into 15 + 2g. Just five symbols. And now you can do things to it — substitute values, compare plans, solve for limits.

Today's job: take English sentences and shrink them into algebra. There are rules — and the trickiest ones are about word order.

🔑 The big idea — every operation word (sum, less, twice, divided by) maps onto a symbol (+, −, ×, ÷). But some operations care about which goes first. "5 less than x" is x − 5 — not 5 − x. Get the order right and the rest is mechanical.

Order free (+ or ×)

💡 Why does order matter for − and ÷? Because a − b ≠ b − a (try 8 − 3 vs 3 − 8). Same for division. But a + b = b + a and a × b = b × a — addition and multiplication don't care.

5Worked example — the 3-step method

Translate into an algebraic expression: "7 less than three times a number n"

1
Find the variable, then translate each operation word
Underline the variable. Box each operation word. Write what each piece means in algebra.
7 less than three times a number n
→ three times n = 3n
→ 7 less than [that] = [that] − 7
2
Watch the word order — write the expression
"7 less than X" means X − 7, not 7 − X. The thing that comes second in words goes first in algebra. Slot in 3n for X.
[that] − 7 where [that] = 3n
= 3n − 7
3
Verify by substituting a value
If n = 4: "three times 4 is 12; 7 less than 12 is 5". Now check the expression: 3(4) − 7 = 12 − 7 = 5. ✓
Words give 5. Expression gives 5. They agree — translation is correct.
Answer 3n − 7
