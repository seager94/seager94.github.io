Circles in First Nations Australian design

First Nations Australian peoples have used circles in many forms over tens of thousands of years.

Basketry. Coiled weaving — practised by Yolŋu and other artists in Arnhem Land and across many regions — builds a vessel from a tight spiral. The spiral has a clear centre and grows outward in even rings.

Symbols. Painted and incised designs from many Country use circles to represent meeting places, water holes, and connections between people and place.

Architecture. Gunyahs (temporary shelters) and ceremonial grounds in many regions use circular or curved footprints, sized to the materials and the gathering.

Each use shares the same maths idea you are learning today: a centre point with every part of the form sitting at a chosen distance from it.

Knowledge belongs to the First Nations Australian peoples whose traditions and ongoing practice it comes from. Generalisations above describe widespread practices, not any single group's design.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. You'll use these five words throughout the lesson.

Centre
Tap to flip
The exact middle point of the circle. Every point on the circle sits the same distance from here.
Marked with a dot in the diagram above.
Radius
Tap to flip
The distance from the centre to any point on the circle. Plural: radii.
A circle with radius 7 cm has every edge point 7 cm from the centre.
Diameter
Tap to flip
A straight line from one side of the circle to the other, passing through the centre.
A diameter is twice the radius. So r = 5 cm gives d = 10 cm.
Chord
Tap to flip
A straight line joining any two points on the circle. A diameter is a special chord — the longest one possible.
Three chords in a circle will usually have three different lengths.
Circumference
Tap to flip
The distance around the outside of the circle. It's a perimeter, but for a circle it has its own name.
If you traced the rim of a plate, the length you drew is the circumference.

5Worked example — radius to diameter

A circle has a radius of 7 cm. What is its diameter?

1
Write down what we know
Identify the given measurement and what we are asked to find.
Given: r = 7 cm
Find: d = ?
2
Apply the relationship
The diameter is always twice the radius — it stretches from one side of the circle to the other through the centre, which is two radii joined end to end.
d = 2 × r
d = 2 × 7
d = 14 cm
3
Check by halving
Half of the diameter should give us back the radius. 14 ÷ 2 = 7 ✓ — matches our given radius, so the answer is correct.
14 ÷ 2 = 7 ✓ — half the diameter equals the radius.
Answer diameter = 14 cm

Three quick sub-steps. After each prompt, discuss with the person next to you, then we'll fill in the box together.

Big idea — the longest chord. A chord is a straight line joining any two points on a circle. The diameter is a special chord — the only one that passes through the centre — and it is always the longest chord possible. Why? Because moving a chord away from the centre shortens it. The diameter sits right through the centre, so no other chord can be longer.
