1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's geometry.

Look at any car park. The white lines marking the bays are parallel — they never meet, no matter how far you extend them. Now imagine a delivery driver wants to cross all the bays at a single angle to drop off a trolley.

The path they walk acts as a transversal — a line that cuts across two or more parallel lines. Every parallel line it crosses produces exactly the same set of angles. That's not a coincidence — it's the geometric fact that lets architects, engineers and surveyors line things up without measuring every single angle.

Today we'll see why those angles repeat, name the three relationships they form, and use them to find missing angles — including angles labelled with algebra. Lessons 08, 09 and 10 all build straight on top of this one, so this is the lesson to get rock solid.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. These five words are the language we'll use for the rest of the unit.

Parallel lines
Tap to flip
Two straight lines that never meet, no matter how far they're extended. Marked with matching arrows.
Like railway tracks or the lines on lined paper.
Transversal
Tap to flip
A line that crosses two or more other lines (usually parallel ones). It creates 8 angles in total — 4 at each intersection.
"Trans-" = across. It cuts across the parallel lines.
Corresponding
Tap to flip
Equal angles in matching positions at each intersection. Form an F shape.
Same corner of each intersection — top-left ↔ top-left.
Alternate
Tap to flip
Equal angles on opposite sides of the transversal, between the parallel lines. Form a Z shape.
"Alternate" = swapping sides of the transversal.
Co-interior
Tap to flip
Angles on the same side of the transversal, between the parallel lines. They add to 180°. Form a C shape.
"Co-" = together. Together they make a straight line's worth.

4Error analysis — the #1 mistake

Read both columns carefully. The student on the left has set up the wrong equation. Work out why before reading the green column.

✗ WrongWhat the student did

Two co-interior angles are labelled (4x + 20)° and (2x − 10)°. Find x.

4x + 20 = 2x − 10
2x = −30
x = −15
The student set the two angles equal — but co-interior angles are not equal. They confused the C rule with the Z rule. A negative answer for an angle measure is a red flag that something has gone wrong.

✓ RightThe fix

Co-interior angles add to 180°. Write a sum, not an equality.

(4x + 20) + (2x − 10) = 180
6x + 10 = 180
6x = 170
x = 853 ≈ 28.3°
Always check first: are the angles equal (corresponding / alternate) or do they sum to 180° (co-interior)? The shape — F, Z, or C — tells you which.

5Worked example — find all 8 angles

In the diagram below, ∠1 = 65°. Find the size of every other angle.

1
Use the angles around point P (the top intersection)
At every intersection, four angles meet. Use angles on a straight line (sum 180°) and vertically opposite angles (equal).
∠1 = 65°   (given)
∠2 = 180° − 65° = 115°   (straight line with ∠1)
∠3 = 65°   (vertically opposite ∠1)
∠4 = 115°   (vertically opposite ∠2)
2
Move down the transversal — use parallel-line rules to reach Q
Pick the most direct rule for each new angle. The fastest way: each angle at Q corresponds to the angle in the matching position at P.
∠5 = 65°   (corresponding to ∠1)
∠6 = 115°   (corresponding to ∠2)
∠7 = 65°   (corresponding to ∠3)
∠8 = 115°   (corresponding to ∠4)
3
Verify with a second rule
Cross-check using alternate (equal) and co-interior (sum 180°).
CheckPairRuleResult
Alternate∠3 & ∠5equal65° = 65° ✓
Alternate∠4 & ∠6equal115° = 115° ✓
Co-interior∠4 & ∠5sum 180°115° + 65° = 180° ✓
Co-interior∠3 & ∠6sum 180°65° + 115° = 180° ✓
Answer — all 8 angles ∠1 = ∠3 = ∠5 = ∠7 = 65°  ·  ∠2 = ∠4 = ∠6 = ∠8 = 115°
