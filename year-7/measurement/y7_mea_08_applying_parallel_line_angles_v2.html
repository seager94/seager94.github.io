1Why this matters — the scissor lift

A real device that hides today's geometry inside its mechanism.

A scissor lift raises workers high up on construction sites. As it extends, its X-shaped crossbars open like a folding gate — but the platform on top stays perfectly level no matter how high it rises.

Look at the picture: can you spot the two parallel lines hiding in the mechanism? And the transversal cutting through them?

A folding umbrella works the same way — its ribs are transversals between two invisible parallel rings. Today we'll calculate the angles inside mechanisms like these, and justify every answer with the property we used.

2Reasons we'll quote today

Tap each card to flip. Today, every angle calculation must finish with a Reason: line naming one of these.

Corresponding angles
Tap to flip
Same position at each crossing on a transversal between parallel lines. They are equal.
F-shape pattern — both angles point the same way.
Alternate angles
Tap to flip
Between parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversal. They are equal.
Z-shape pattern — angles are tucked into the two corners of the Z.
Co-interior angles
Tap to flip
Between parallel lines, on the same side of the transversal. They sum to 180°.
C-shape pattern — the two angles trap the transversal between the parallels.
Vertically opposite
Tap to flip
Two lines crossing make an X. The angles diagonally across are equal.
No parallel lines needed — works at any crossing.
Angles on a straight line
Tap to flip
Two angles that share a vertex and sit along one straight line sum to 180°.
Also called a linear pair.

5Worked example 1 — scissor lift

A diagonal crossbar makes a 35° angle with the base of a scissor lift. Find angles a and b at the platform — and give a reason for each.

The platform and base stay parallel as the lift extends. The crossbar is the transversal.
1
Identify the structure
Spot the parallel lines and the transversal before reaching for a property.
Parallel lines: PLATFORM and BASE
Transversal: crossbar
Given: angle at base = 35°
Reason Two parallel lines crossed by a single transversal — we can quote any parallel-line angle property at either intersection.
2
Find angle a
Angle a sits between the parallels, on the opposite side of the crossbar to the 35° angle — that's a Z-shape.
a = 35°
Reason Alternate angles between parallel lines are equal.
3
Find angle b
Angles a and b sit next to each other along the platform line — they form a linear pair.
a + b = 180°
35° + b = 180°
b = 145°
Reason Angles on a straight line sum to 180°.
Answer a = 35°, b = 145°

7Worked example 2 — exterior angle of a triangle

Triangle ABC has side BC extended to D, so angle ACD is an exterior angle. We'll show that the exterior angle equals the sum of the two non-adjacent interior angles α and β — and the proof uses only parallel-line properties.

Solid: triangle ABC. Dashed: extension to D, and the constructed line CE through C, parallel to AB.
1
Construct a line through C parallel to AB
Through vertex C, draw line CE parallel to side AB. Now AB ∥ CE — two parallel lines we can quote properties about.
AB  ∥  CE  (constructed)
Transversal 1: AC   Transversal 2: BD
Reason A line parallel to a given line can be drawn through any external point — this is a valid construction.
2
Split the exterior angle into two parts
At C, the constructed line CE divides the exterior angle into two pieces: α' (between AC and CE) and β' (between CE and CD).
α' = α  (angles BAC and ACE)
β' = β  (angles ABC and ECD)
Reason Alternate angles (AB ∥ CE, transversal AC) give α' = α. Corresponding angles (AB ∥ CE, transversal BD) give β' = β.
3
Add the two parts to get the exterior angle
The exterior angle at C is the sum of those two pieces — so it equals the sum of the two non-adjacent interior angles.
exterior angle ACD = α' + β'
                 = α + β
Reason Exterior angle of a triangle = sum of the two non-adjacent interior angles. (Just proved, from alternate + corresponding angles.)
New rule exterior angle = sum of the two non-adjacent interior angles
