1Why this matters — the string experiment

Imagine you wrap string around three round objects, then measure the diameter across. Watch what happens when you divide.

Take three round objects — a jar lid, a dinner plate, and a bike wheel. For each one:

  1. Wrap a piece of string once around the edge. Mark the string where it meets itself — that length is the circumference (C).
  2. Lay a ruler straight across the middle to get the diameter (d).
  3. Divide C by d.

Something strange happens. The numbers C and d are different for every object — but C ÷ d is always about the same.

ObjectC (cm)d (cm)C ÷ d
Jar lid25.183.14
Dinner plate87.9283.14
Bike wheel206.065.63.14
Three different circles, three identical ratios. That repeating number — about 3.14 — is what we're meeting today. It has a name: π (pi).

Diameter
Tap to flip
The distance straight across a circle through its centre. We write it as d.
A ruler placed across a plate through its middle measures d.
Ratio
Tap to flip
A comparison between two quantities, often shown as a division.
C ÷ d is the ratio of circumference to diameter.
Constant
Tap to flip
A value that never changes — no matter the situation.
C ÷ d is the same constant for every circle that ever existed.
π (pi)
Tap to flip
The Greek letter we use for the constant C ÷ d.

Probably a measurement error

💡 For an accurate circle measurement, C ÷ d should land near 3.14. If the ratio is well below 3 or well above 3.3, something went wrong — the string slipped, or d was measured off-centre.

4How did people first find π?

Long before calculators, many civilisations tried to pin down this strange ratio. Their answers all hover near 3.14 — even though they were working independently, in different centuries, on different continents.

Egypt
≈ 1650 BCE · Rhind Papyrus
≈ 3.16
An ancient papyrus scroll shows scribes treating the area of a circle as the square of 89 of the diameter — which corresponds to π ≈ 3.16.
Babylon
≈ 1900 BCE · Clay tablets
≈ 3.125
Babylonian mathematicians, working with clay tablets and a base-60 number system, used π ≈ 318.
Greece (Archimedes)
≈ 250 BCE
3 + 1071 < π < 3 + 17
Archimedes drew polygons inside and outside a circle, then squeezed π between two fractions: between roughly 3.1408 and 3.1429.
India (Aryabhata)
≈ 499 CE
3.1416
The astronomer-mathematician Aryabhata gave π ≈ 3.1416 — accurate to four decimal places.
China (Zu Chongzhi)
≈ 480 CE
355113 ≈ 3.14159292
Zu Chongzhi found a fraction that matches π to six decimal places — a record that stood for nearly a thousand years.

5Worked example — finding C ÷ d

A circle has circumference C = 31.4 cm and diameter d = 10 cm. Find C ÷ d and compare it to π.

1
Write the ratio
We want the value of C ÷ d — circumference divided by diameter.
C ÷ d = 31.4 ÷ 10
2
Divide
Dividing by 10 shifts the decimal point one place to the left.
C ÷ d = 31.4 ÷ 10 = 3.14
3
Compare to π
We know that π ≈ 3.14159... Our ratio is 3.14 — within rounding, this is π. The match isn't a coincidence: every circle gives this ratio.
3.14 ≈ π   ✓
Answer C ÷ d = 3.14 ≈ π
