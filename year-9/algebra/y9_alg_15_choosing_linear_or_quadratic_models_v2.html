1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's algebra.

Imagine you're starting a side business — a weekend lawn-mowing service. Two questions land in your inbox on the same day:

  • "You charge a $40 call-out fee plus $25 per lawn. What will 6 lawns cost?"
  • "If you raise your price, you'll mow fewer lawns. What price gives you the most profit?"

Both questions are about money. But they need different shapes of model — one is a straight line, the other is a curve with a peak.

Today you'll learn to spot the difference from a small table of values. Constant first differences → straight line (linear). Constant second differences → curve (quadratic). Same financial story, but pick the wrong model and the prediction will be way off.

Linear function

A function whose graph is a straight line. The output changes by the same amount each step.
y = 5x + 2  goes up by 5 each time.
Quadratic function
Tap to flip
A function whose graph is a parabola — a U-shape or upside-down-U with a single peak or trough.
y = −x² + 10x  has a maximum.
First difference
Tap to flip
The change between consecutive outputs. If first differences are constant → linear.
For 3, 7, 11, 15: first diffs are 4, 4, 4.
Second difference
Tap to flip
The change between consecutive first differences. If second differences are constant → quadratic.
For 1, 4, 9, 16: 1st diffs 3, 5, 7. 2nd diffs 2, 2.
Parabola
Tap to flip
The U-shaped curve a quadratic draws. In profit problems it usually opens downwards — there's a best price.
Profit vs price: rises, peaks, falls.

5Worked example — a trade quote

A plumber charges an $80 call-out fee plus $60 per hour. We're given costs for the first few hours and asked: linear or quadratic?

Data table

hours h01234
cost C ($)80140200260320
1st diff—+60+60+60+60

Graph

1
Find the first differences
Subtract each cost from the next. If they all match, the rate of change is constant.
140 − 80 = 60   200 − 140 = 60   260 − 200 = 60   320 − 260 = 60
2
Read the pattern
First differences are all +60. That's the signal for a linear model — cost grows at a steady rate of $60 per hour.
Constant first difference → linear model
Rule: C = 80 + 60h
3
Interpret in context and check
The $80 is the call-out fee (cost when h = 0). The 60 is the hourly rate. Predict cost for 5 hours: 80 + 60(5) = 380. The plot is a straight line — agrees with the table.
C(5) = 80 + 60 × 5 = 380
Answer Linear model: C = 80 + 60h
🔑 The rule of thumb: If first differences are constant, choose a linear model. The straight-line graph confirms it.

6Compare two solutions — a profit problem

A school stall sells cupcakes. The price p (in dollars) affects how many they sell, so revenue = price × quantity. The table below shows profit at different prices.

Data table — profit at price p

price p ($)0123456
profit P ($)03664849610096
1st diff—+36+28+20+12+4−4
2nd diff——−8−8−8−8−8

Graph

Yusuf
"It's linear."

"Profit goes up steadily as the price rises, so it's a straight line. I'd write P = 20p — that fits the start of the table well enough."

Maddy
"It's quadratic."

"When the price rises, fewer people buy. So revenue is price × quantity, and that gives a parabola with a maximum. The profit can't go up forever — at some price, sales drop."

Let's settle it using the differences in the table. Work through the three prompts with the person next to you — we'll fill them in together.

💡 Decision rule: The first differences are not constant (36, 28, 20, …) — so it isn't linear. The second differences are constant (−8 every time) — so it's quadratic. Maddy is right. The graph also rises, peaks at p = $5, then falls — the classic parabola shape.
