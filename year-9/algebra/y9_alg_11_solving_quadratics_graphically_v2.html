1Why this matters

From the lunar cycle to the maths we'll learn today.

Watch the moon over a month. It moves from a thin crescent to half, to nearly full, to full — then back the other way. The fraction of the moon that's lit up rises smoothly and then falls smoothly. If you plot brightness against the day of the cycle, the curve peaks in the middle and dips on either side — the same kind of smooth, symmetric shape as a parabola.

Today we ask the question that comes back again and again with parabolas: where does the curve hit zero? That's the same as solving a quadratic equation.

A quadratic equation is one like x2 − x − 6 = 0. We'll find its solutions two ways: by reading the x-intercepts off a graph, and by narrowing in with a table of values until the answer is precise to several decimal places.
First Nations Australians have observed and tracked the lunar cycle for tens of thousands of years — using its rhythm for seasonal timing, navigation, food gathering and ceremony. The smooth rise and fall of moonlight across a month is one of many natural patterns that mathematicians today model with curves like parabolas.

Quadratic equation

An equation with an x2 term (and no higher power of x).

Parabola
Tap to flip
The U-shaped curve you get when you plot a quadratic relation y = x2 + …
y = x2 − 4 plots as a parabola with its lowest point at (0, −4).
Root (solution)
Tap to flip
A value of x that makes the quadratic equal to zero. Also called a zero of the equation.
x = 3 is a root of x2 − x − 6 = 0 because 9 − 3 − 6 = 0.
x-intercept
Tap to flip
A point where the graph crosses the x-axis. Its y-value is 0.
The x-intercepts of y = x2 − x − 6 are at x = −2 and x = 3.
Sign change
Tap to flip
When y goes from negative to positive (or back) between two x-values, a root lies between them.
If y = −0.56 at x = 6.2 and y = +0.69 at x = 6.3, a root sits between 6.2 and 6.3.

5Worked example 1 — graphical method

Solve: x2 − x − 6 = 0

Figure 1 — the parabola y = x2 − x − 6. The two filled circles mark the x-intercepts (roots).
1
Re-read the question as "where does y = 0?"
Solving x2 − x − 6 = 0 means finding the x-values that make the expression equal 0. If we set y = x2 − x − 6, the solutions are the x-values where y = 0 — i.e., where the parabola crosses the x-axis.
Solutions of x2 − x − 6 = 0  ⇔  x-intercepts of y = x2 − x − 6
2
Read the x-intercepts off the graph
From the figure above, the parabola cuts the x-axis at two clean integer values.
Left crossing: x = −2    Right crossing: x = 3
3
Verify by substituting back
A good habit — check both candidate roots make the equation true.
x = −2:   (−2)2 − (−2) − 6 = 4 + 2 − 6 = 0 ✓
x = 3:   (3)2 − (3) − 6 = 9 − 3 − 6 = 0 ✓
Solutions x = −2  or  x = 3

6Worked example 2 — numerical method

Solve: x2 = 39, to three decimal places.

Figure 2 — the parabola y = x2 − 39. The roots fall between integers, so we'll find them with a table.
1
Rewrite the equation as "y = 0"
To use the graph idea, move everything to one side so we're looking for x-intercepts.
x2 = 39  ⇒  x2 − 39 = 0  ⇒  solutions are the x-intercepts of y = x2 − 39.
2
Find the sign change between integers
Build a table of values for y = x2 − 39. Look for two consecutive x-values where y switches from negative to positive — a root must lie between them.

The root sits between 6.244 and 6.245, so to three decimal places x ≈ 6.245. By the symmetry of the parabola, the other root is x ≈ −6.245.
Solutions (to 3 d.p.) x ≈ ±6.245
💡 Why this works: a continuous curve can't jump from below the x-axis to above without crossing through zero. So whenever the sign of y flips between two x-values, there must be a root in that gap. Narrowing the gap narrows the answer.
