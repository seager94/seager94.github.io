1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's maths.

You kick a football into the air. Its height above the ground over time traces a smooth, symmetric arc — up to a peak, then back down to the grass.

That arc isn't random. It's a parabola, the U-shaped curve of a quadratic function. A coach analysing a kick reads four key landmarks from the curve: when the ball leaves the boot (a y-intercept), how high it gets (the vertex), when it lands (an x-intercept), and the axis of symmetry that splits "going up" from "coming down."

Today we learn how to spot a quadratic from its rule, its table, or its graph — and how to read those four landmarks.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. You'll use these five words constantly today.

Quadratic function
Tap to flip
A function whose rule has an x² term as the highest power, in the form y = ax² + bx + c.
y = x² + 2x − 3 is quadratic.
Parabola
Tap to flip
The U-shaped curve that is the graph of a quadratic function. It is symmetric about a vertical line.
Opens up if the coefficient of x² is positive; down if negative.
Vertex
Tap to flip
The turning point — the single lowest or highest point on the parabola. It sits exactly on the axis of symmetry.
For y = x² − 4: vertex is (0, −4).
Axis of symmetry
Tap to flip
The vertical line that cuts the parabola into mirror-image halves. It passes through the vertex.
For y = (x − 2)²: axis is x = 2.
Intercepts
Tap to flip
Where the graph crosses the axes. The y-intercept is where x = 0; the x-intercepts are where y = 0.
For y = x² − 4: y-int −4; x-ints −2, 2.

3Spotting a quadratic from a table

For each table, look at the differences between consecutive y-values. A constant first difference means linear. A constant second difference means quadratic. Neither means it's something else. Pick the right category for each — feedback is instant.

💡 Why second differences? For y = ax² + bx + c with x stepping by 1, the second differences are always 2a — a constant. That's the algebraic signature of a quadratic.

4Multiple representations — match rule to vertex

Each quadratic rule has one matching vertex. Click two chips that go together. There are five pairs. After today, you'll be able to do this just from looking at the rule.

Pick a chip to start matching.
Tip: a rule of the form y = (x − h)² + k has vertex (h, k) — the brackets tell you the horizontal shift, the constant tells you the vertical shift.

Example 4 — combined shifts: y = x² + 2x − 3

This is the showcase example — work it from table to plot to features. Reveal each step in turn.

1
Build a table of values
Substitute x = −4 through 2 into y = x² + 2x − 3. Then check the second differences to confirm it's quadratic.

2

Plot each (x, y) from the table and join them with a smooth U-curve. The lowest point sits at x = −1.

3
Read off the four key features
Find them on the graph, then double-check with the rule.
Vertex: (−1, −4) — the lowest point of the U.
Axis of symmetry: x = −1 — the vertical line through the vertex.
y-intercept: −3 — where the curve crosses the y-axis. Check: sub x = 0 → 0 + 0 − 3 = −3 ✓
x-intercepts: −3 and 1 — where the curve crosses the x-axis. Check: (x + 3)(x − 1) = 0 gives x = −3 or 1 ✓
