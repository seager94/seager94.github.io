1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's algebra.

An engineer is designing a curved skateboard ramp. The cross-section is a parabola — the same U-shape you get from y = x2.

But the engineer needs to steer the curve: make it wider so the ramp is gentler, slide its base sideways so the lowest point lines up with the centre of the bowl, and lift the whole curve up off the ground so it sits on a platform.

Each of those changes is a parameter adjustment — a small letter in the rule that controls one feature of the curve.

Today's rule looks like y = a(x − h)2 + k. Three letters, three jobs: a sets the steepness and which way it opens, h slides it sideways, k lifts it up or down. Change one, watch what happens. Generalise.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. You'll use these five words constantly today.

Parabola
Tap to flip
The U-shaped curve made by any rule of the form y = ax2 + ...
The simplest one is y = x2 — every other parabola today is a transformed version of it.
Turning point
Tap to flip
The highest or lowest point of a parabola — where it changes direction. Also called the vertex.
For y = x2: turning point is (0, 0). For y = (x − 3)2 + 1: it's at (3, 1).
Completed-square form
Tap to flip
y = a(x − h)2 + k. A way of writing a quadratic that reveals the turning point directly.
Reading y = 2(x − 4)2 − 1: a = 2, h = 4, k = −1.
Translation
Tap to flip
A slide — moves the whole graph without changing its shape. Sideways slides come from h; up/down slides come from k.
y = (x − 5)2 + 2 is the base parabola translated 5 right, 2 up.
Reflection
Tap to flip
A flip. When a is negative, the parabola is reflected over the x-axis — it now opens downward.
y = −x2 is the base parabola flipped upside down.

Generalisations to write in your book

a
When |a| > 1, the parabola is narrower (steeper). When 0 < |a| < 1, it is wider (flatter). When a < 0, it is reflected over the x-axis — it opens downward.
h
Changing h translates the parabola horizontally. The turning point moves to x = h. Watch the sign: (x − 3)2 shifts right by 3; (x + 3)2 = (x − (−3))2 shifts left by 3.
k
Changing k translates the parabola vertically. The turning point moves to y = k. Positive k lifts the curve up; negative k drops it down.
TP
Put it all together — the turning point sits at (h, k). That's the headline result of completed-square form: the turning point is hiding in plain sight.

Worked examples — one parameter at a time

Each card holds x, h or k still and varies the remaining one. The grey dashed curve is always y = x2.

Effect of a — dilation

y = 2x2
Turning point: (0, 0)
a = 2 doubles every y-value, so the parabola is twice as steep — narrower than the base. Turning point stays at the origin because h = 0 and k = 0.

Effect of a — reflection

y = −x2
Turning point: (0, 0) — now a maximum
a = −1 flips every y-value to its opposite. The parabola is reflected over the x-axis — same width, but opens downward.

Effect of h — horizontal shift

y = (x − 3)2
Turning point: (3, 0)
h = 3 moves the whole parabola 3 units right. The minus sign inside the bracket is part of the form — (x − 3) is zero when x = 3, so the turning point lands at x = 3.

Effect of k — vertical shift

y = x2 + 4
Turning point: (0, 4)
k = 4 lifts the whole parabola 4 units up. Every y-value of the base curve gains 4. Shape is unchanged.

Combined — all three at once

y = −(x − 2)2 + 5
Turning point: (2, 5) — a maximum
a = −1 → reflected (opens downward). h = 2 → 2 units right. k = 5 → 5 units up. Read the turning point straight off the rule: (h, k) = (2, 5).
