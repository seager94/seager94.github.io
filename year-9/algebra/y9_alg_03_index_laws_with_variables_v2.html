Three reminders of the index laws on numerical bases — the rules you already know. Today we'll use them on algebraic terms.

1Why this matters

Same laws — now with letters in the base.

In the last two lessons you used the index laws on numerical bases:

23 × 25 = 28   ·   7672 = 74   ·   (52)3 = 56

Replace the base with a letter — the rule doesn't change. x3 × x5 = x8. Same law, different base.

Today's twist: most algebraic terms come with a coefficient in front, like 3x2. The big idea — handle the coefficient and the variable part separately. The coefficients use the operation in front of you (× or ÷). The variables use the index law.

5Worked example — handle the coefficient and the variable separately

Simplify: 3x2 × 4x3

Product law
xm × xn = xm+n
Quotient law
xm ÷ xn = xm−n
Power of a power
(xm)n = xmn
1
Split the term — coefficient × variable part
Each term has a number and a variable-with-exponent. Group the numbers together and the variables together.
3 × x2 × 4 × x3
= (3 × 4) × (x2 × x3)
2
Coefficients: multiply. Variables: apply the product law (add exponents).
The × between the terms is a regular multiplication for the coefficients. The product law adds the exponents on the matching base.
= 12 × x2+3
= 12x5
TrapTwo common errors here:
  • Adding the coefficients instead of multiplying — writing 7x5. The × in front means multiply: 3 × 4 = 12, not 3 + 4.
  • Multiplying the exponents instead of adding — writing 12x6. The product law adds exponents (2 + 3 = 5). You only multiply exponents for a power-of-a-power.
3
Check by expanding
Write the powers out as repeated multiplication and count.
3x2 × 4x3 = 3·x·x × 4·x·x·x = (3 × 4)·x·x·x·x·x = 12x5 ✓
Answer 12x5

5bWorked example — quotient law

Simplify: 12a5 ÷ 3a2

1
Split — coefficient ÷ coefficient, variable ÷ variable
12a5 ÷ 3a2 = (12 ÷ 3) × (a5 ÷ a2)
2
Coefficients: divide. Variables: subtract exponents.
= 4 × a5−2 = 4a3
TrapDon't subtract the coefficients. The ÷ between the terms divides 12 by 3 — it doesn't make the coefficient 12 − 3 = 9.
Answer 4a3

5cWorked example — power of a product

Simplify: (2x)3

1
The bracket applies to every factor inside
The exponent 3 attaches to both the 2 and the x. This is the most-missed step of the whole topic.
(2x)3 = 23 × x3
2
Evaluate the numeric power; leave the variable power as is
= 8x3
TrapA frequent error is writing 2x3 — only cubing the x and leaving the 2. The bracket says everything inside gets the exponent. 23 = 8, not 2.
Answer 8x3

5dWorked example — combining laws with multi-variable terms

Simplify: (2xy)3 ÷ (xy4)

1
Expand the bracket — distribute the exponent to every factor
(2xy)3 = 23 × x3 × y3 = 8x3y3
2
Rewrite the quotient, then apply the quotient law per base
Note x in the denominator means x1.
8x3y3xy4 = 8 × x3−1 × y3−4 = 8x2y−1
3
A negative index — what does it mean?
A negative exponent means a reciprocal: y−1 = 1y. Either form is acceptable today; in this lesson we'll typically leave the negative-index form.
= 8x2y−1  or equivalently  8x2y
Answer 8x2y−1
