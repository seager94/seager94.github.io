1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's algebra.

When you throw a ball straight up, its height doesn't change at a constant rate. Gravity pulls it: it slows as it rises, pauses for an instant at the top, then speeds up as it falls. The path is a parabola.

The exact same shape appears in surprising places — the area enclosed by a fixed length of fencing, the cross-section of a satellite dish, even the catch-rate of a cooperative hunting drive. Today we learn to build, read, and judge models that follow this curve.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. You'll use these five words constantly today.

Parabola
Tap to flip
The smooth U-shape drawn by every quadratic function — sometimes opening up, sometimes down.
y = x2 draws a parabola with its lowest point at (0, 0).
Quadratic function
Tap to flip
A function of the form y = ax2 + bx + c, where a ≠ 0.
h(t) = −5t2 + 20t models a ball's height.
Turning point
Tap to flip
The point where a parabola changes direction — the maximum (if it opens down) or minimum (if it opens up).
For h(t) = −5t2 + 20t, the turning point is (2, 20) — max height 20 m at 2 s.
Intercept
Tap to flip
Where the graph crosses an axis. The x-intercepts have y = 0; the y-intercept has x = 0.
y = x2 − 4 has x-intercepts at (−2, 0) and (2, 0), y-intercept (0, −4).
Mathematical model
Tap to flip
A maths description (formula or graph) of a real situation, used to predict and interpret.
A(w) = −w2 + 20w models the area of a paddock with 40 m of fencing.

Linear — constant rate of change

Quadratic — curved (parabola)

💡 Linear change adds a constant amount each step. Quadratic change is curved — the rate of change is itself changing, like a ball slowing as it rises and then accelerating as it falls.

5Worked example — a netball shot

Estimate the maximum height before we compute it. Then we'll walk through the method, step by step.

🏐 A netball player launches the ball straight up at 20 m/s from ground level. After t seconds the height (in metres) is modelled by h(t) = −5t2 + 20t. How high does it reach, and when does it land?
🪜 Estimation ladder — commit before computing
First, gut feel: what's the maximum height the ball reaches? Don't compute — just commit a guess.
1
Identify a, b, c and the shape
Compare to y = ax2 + bx + c. The sign of a tells you whether the parabola opens up or down.
a = −5, b = 20, c = 0.
a < 0 → parabola opens downward → the turning point is a maximum.
2
Find the turning point (max height + time)
For a parabola, the turning point sits at x = −b2a. Then substitute back to get the height.
t = −202 × (−5) = −20−10 = 2 s
h(2) = −5(2)2 + 20(2) = −20 + 40 = 20 m
Turning point: (2, 20) — maximum height 20 m at t = 2 s.
3
Find the intercepts and interpret in context
The x-intercepts (where h = 0) tell us when the ball is at ground level. Factorise to find them.
0 = −5t2 + 20t = −5t(t − 4)
→ t = 0 or t = 4.
Interpret: at t = 0 the ball leaves her hand; at t = 4 it returns to ground. Total air time: 4 s.
Answer Max height 20 m at t = 2 s; the ball lands at t = 4 s.

6Together — fencing a paddock

We have 40 m of fencing to enclose a rectangular paddock. Let w = the width (m). Then the length is (20 − w) and the area is A(w) = w(20 − w) = −w2 + 20w. Estimate the best width first, then we'll compute.

🪜 Estimation ladder — what's the biggest area?
Gut feel: what's the largest area you can enclose with 40 m of fencing? Commit a guess before we compute.
💡 Notice the pattern: when the area equation is −w2 + 20w, the maximum is reached exactly halfway between the two intercepts (0 and 20). This is true for every parabola — the turning point sits on the vertical line of symmetry.

First Nations Australians' cooperative hunting drives. Many First Nations peoples used coordinated drives — a small group of skilled hunters guiding game (kangaroos, emus, fish) toward a chosen kill zone. Anthropological accounts of these techniques suggest the yield from a drive rises with the number of hunters up to an optimum, then falls as too many people disturb the animals or get in each other's way. This pattern is well modelled by a downward-opening quadratic.
