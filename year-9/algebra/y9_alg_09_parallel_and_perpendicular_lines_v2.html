1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's algebra.

Look at the streets in any planned city — Adelaide, New York, Melbourne's CBD. The map is a grid because surveyors had a strict rule:

  • Streets running the same direction must be parallel 🟧
  • Cross streets must meet them at a right angle 🟦

On a Cartesian plane, every street is described by a linear equation. Two parallel streets have the same gradient. Two streets meeting at a right angle have gradients that pass a very specific test.

Yesterday we worked out the gradient of a line using riserun or by reading m from y = mx + c. Today we use those gradients to compare two lines — and decide whether they're parallel, perpendicular, or neither.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. You'll use these five words constantly today.

Gradient
Tap to flip
The steepness of a line — rise over run. In y = mx + c, the gradient is m.
In y = 3x + 2, the gradient is 3.
Parallel
Tap to flip
Two lines that never meet — they keep the same distance apart forever. They have equal gradients.
y = 2x + 1 and y = 2x − 4 are parallel. Both have m = 2.
Perpendicular
Tap to flip
Two lines that meet at a right angle (90°). Their gradients multiply to give −1.
y = 2x and y = −12x are perpendicular. 2 × −12 = −1.
Reciprocal
Tap to flip
A number's reciprocal is 1 divided by it — flip the fraction.
The reciprocal of 4 is 14. The reciprocal of 23 is 32.
Negative reciprocal
Tap to flip
Flip the fraction and change the sign. This is the gradient of a perpendicular line.
The negative reciprocal of 3 is −13. Of −25: 52.

5Worked examples — the gradient method

Two checks. Same routine for both: (1) read the gradient of each line, (2) compare them with the right test, (3) conclude. The grid on the left of each example shows what the relationship looks like.

Example A — Parallel?

Decide whether y = 2x + 1 and y = 2x − 3 are parallel.

1
Read each gradient from y = mx + c
The coefficient of x is the gradient.
m1 = 2  (from y = 2x + 1)
m2 = 2  (from y = 2x − 3)
2
Apply the parallel test: m1 = m2?
Parallel lines have equal gradients.
2 = 2 ✓
3
Conclude
Same gradient means the lines rise at the same rate but with different y-intercepts (1 vs −3) — so they never meet.
The lines are parallel.
Answer Yes — parallel (both have gradient 2)
Example B — Perpendicular?

Decide whether y = 3x + 2 and y = −13x + 5 are perpendicular.

1
Read each gradient
Pick the coefficient of x from each equation.
m1 = 3  (from y = 3x + 2)
m2 = −13  (from y = −13x + 5)
2
Apply the perpendicular test: m1 × m2 = −1?
Perpendicular lines have gradients whose product is −1 — each is the negative reciprocal of the other.
3 × (−13) = −33 = −1 ✓
3
Conclude
Notice that −13 is the negative reciprocal of 3 — flip 3 to 13, then change the sign. The lines meet at exactly 90°.
The lines are perpendicular.
Answer Yes — perpendicular (gradients 3 and −13)
⚠ The trap A negative gradient is NOT the same as perpendicular. Many students see a positive gradient and a negative gradient and assume the lines must meet at 90°. They don't, unless the gradients are negative reciprocals — that is, unless m1 × m2 = −1.
For example, y = 2x and y = −2x are not perpendicular — their gradients multiply to give −4, not −1. They're a reflection of one another about the y-axis, but the angle between them is not 90°. Always multiply and check.

6Together — two more cases

First a "find the gradient" task, then a sneaky "neither" case. Same gradient method as the I Do.

Example C — Find a perpendicular gradient

A line has gradient 4. What is the gradient of any line perpendicular to it?

1
Recall the rule
For perpendicular lines, m1 × m2 = −1, so m2 = −1m1 (the negative reciprocal).
If m1 = 4, then m2 = −14
2
Read it as "flip and change the sign"
4 is the same as 41. Flip to 14. Change the sign: −14.
4  →  14  →  −14
3
Check by multiplying
Always finish by multiplying — the product should be −1.
4 × (−14) = −44 = −1 ✓
Answer m2 = −14
Example D — A "neither" case

Are y = 2x and y = 3x parallel, perpendicular, or neither?

1
Read each gradient
m1 = 2,   m2 = 3
2
Parallel? Check m1 = m2
2 ≠ 3  →  not parallel
3
Perpendicular? Check m1 × m2 = −1
Both lines pass through the origin, so they certainly meet — but the question is whether at 90°.
2 × 3 = 6, not −1  →  not perpendicular
Answer Neither — different gradients, product is not −1
💡 Routine to follow on every question: read each gradient → test parallel (equal?) → test perpendicular (product = −1?) → if neither test passes, the answer is "neither".
