1Why this matters

Real situations turn into linear functions every day.

Your mum puts a 1.8 kg roast in the oven for Sunday lunch. The recipe book says:

  • 30 minutes of resting at the end
  • 45 minutes of cooking per kilogram

How long until dinner? You could guess… or you could write a rule linking weight to total time, draw the graph, and read off the answer for any roast.

That rule is a linear function: total time = 30 + 45 × weight, or T = 30 + 45w. Today we'll build rules like this for cooking, draining tanks and rectangles — graph them, and check they actually make sense.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. These five words frame the whole lesson.

Mathematical model
Tap to flip
A rule or equation that represents a real situation using maths.
T = 30 + 45w models total cooking time for a roast of weight w kg.
Linear function
Tap to flip
A function of the form y = mx + c. Its graph is a straight line.
y = 2x + 5 is linear. Its graph rises steadily.
Gradient (rate of change)
Tap to flip
The constant rate the output changes for every 1 unit of input — the slope of the line.
In T = 30 + 45w, the gradient is 45: 45 more minutes per extra kg.
y-intercept
Tap to flip
The starting value — where the line crosses the vertical axis, the output when input = 0.
In T = 30 + 45w, the y-intercept is 30: the fixed resting time.
Valid range
Tap to flip
The set of input values for which the model gives sensible answers.
For the roast model, valid w > 0 kg — and probably less than 5 kg.

3The modelling cycle

Every model we build today follows the same four moves. Memorise the order — every You Do problem expects all four.

1
Formulate
Pull the starting value and the rate out of the situation. Write y = mx + c.
2
Graph
Draw the line on labelled axes. Mark the y-intercept and one key point.
3
Interpret
Say what the gradient and y-intercept mean in the situation — with units.
4
Check
Is the answer reasonable? What's the valid range? Are the assumptions OK?
💡 The whole lesson is one repeated cycle. Different situations, same four steps. Get the rhythm now and the rest is just substitution.

6Worked example — cooking a roast

A roast needs 30 minutes of resting plus 45 minutes of cooking per kilogram. Build the model, graph it, interpret it, check it.

1
Formulate the rule
Identify the fixed part (the resting period) and the rate (minutes per kg). Let w = weight in kg, T = total time in minutes.
Fixed part: 30 min (the y-intercept)
Rate: 45 min per kg (the gradient)
Rule: T = 30 + 45w
2
Graph the model
Plot w across, T up. Mark the y-intercept (0, 30) and one key point — at w = 2 kg, T = 30 + 90 = 120 min.
Figure: T = 30 + 45w. The line starts at 30 min and rises by 45 min per extra kg.
3
Interpret and check
Say what the numbers mean in context, then sanity-check.
y-intercept (30): if a roast had 0 kg, you'd still wait 30 min — that's the resting time alone.
Gradient (45): for every extra kg, the total time grows by 45 minutes.
Check: for a 1.8 kg roast, T = 30 + 45(1.8) = 30 + 81 = 111 min ≈ 1 h 51 min. Reasonable for Sunday lunch ✓
Valid range: w > 0 (you need some meat), and realistically w < 5 kg (a 100 kg "roast" makes no sense).
Model T = 30 + 45w  ·  gradient 45 min/kg, y-intercept 30 min

Figure: V = 200 − 8t. The line starts at 200 L and drops by 8 L per minute, reaching 0 at t = 25.
💡 Notice the negative gradient. Anything that decreases at a constant rate has a negative slope on the graph — the line goes down as time goes on.
