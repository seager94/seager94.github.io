1Why this matters

A real decision that is secretly today's statistics.

Mia, 15, has two casual job offers. She asked 11 workers at each place what they earned last week.

Illustrative data made for this lesson.

Which job pays better? Look where each group of dots sits, how spread out it is, and its shape. Supermarket pay is bunched tightly around $150. Café pay is usually lower, but it has a long tail of big weeks (tips and extra shifts). A single number can't capture that difference. Putting the two dot plots on the same scale, one above the other, lets you compare centre, spread and shape at a glance. Today you learn to read and compare two distributions using parallel dot plots and back-to-back stem-and-leaf plots.

💡 Skew is named after its tail, not its peak. If a few unusually high values drag a tail out to the right, it's positive skew. If a few unusually low values drag it to the left, it's negative skew. Two peaks usually means two different groups have been mixed together.

5Worked example — the 3-step method

A Year 9 class measured their heights. Compare the girls' and boys' heights using centre, spread and shape.

Key: 2 | 15 | 0 means 152 cm for a girl and 150 cm for a boy

Read the left side outwards from the stem: on the 15 row, the girls' leaves 9 8 6 5 3 1 mean 151, 153, 155, 156, 158 and 159.

1
Centre: find each median
Each group has 15 students, so the median is the 8th value when counted in order from the smallest.
Girls: 148, 151, 153, 155, 156, 158, 159, 160, … so the median is 160 cm.
Boys: 150, 154, 158, 161, 163, 165, 167, 168, … so the median is 168 cm.
The boys' median is 168 − 160 = 8 cm higher.
2
Spread: find each range
Range = largest value − smallest value. Read the ends of each side.
Girls: 172 − 148 = 24 cm
Boys: 185 − 150 = 35 cm
The boys' heights are more spread out.
3
Shape: describe, then write the comparison
Look at the length of each row of leaves, like a bar chart on its side.
Girls: rows of 1, 6, 7, 1 leaves: peak in the middle, tapering both ways, so roughly symmetric.
Boys: rows of 3, 5, 6, 1 leaves: also roughly symmetric, centred higher, with a slightly longer tail towards the shorter heights.
ComparisonBoys were typically taller (median 168 cm vs 160 cm) and their heights were more spread out (range 35 cm vs 24 cm). Both distributions are roughly symmetric.

💡 The outlier claims are the key ones. A value far above the rest always stretches the range and always pulls the mean up, but the median only moves a little, or not at all: 1, 2, 2, 3 has median 2, and 1, 2, 2, 3, 100 still has median 2. That is why the median is the safer measure of centre when a distribution has an outlier or a long tail.

7bHinge check: read the comparison🎯 Pick one

Pick the one correct statement. Each wrong option hides a common mistake, so your choice tells us what to revisit before you start your tier.

Year 7 and Year 9 students recorded their screen time on a school day.
Skew is named by the tail, not the peak. Year 7's peak is on the left at 2 hours, and its long thin tail stretches right to 7 hours. A tail to the right is positive skew.
Correct. Year 7's tail stretches to the right, so it is positively skewed. Its median is 2 hours (6th and 7th values are both 2), while Year 9's is 4.5 hours.
A tall stack shows how many, not how much. The height of a stack is a frequency. The centre is about where the data sits on the scale: Year 7's median is 2 hours, Year 9's is 4.5 hours.
Spread is about the values, not the count. Year 7 runs from 1 to 7 hours (range 6) and Year 9 from 2 to 7 hours (range 5), so the spreads differ even though both groups have 12 students.
