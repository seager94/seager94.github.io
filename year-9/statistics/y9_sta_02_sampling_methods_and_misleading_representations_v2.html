1Why this matters

A real situation that is secretly today's statistics.

Bank B's online advert for its savings account: “Our rate leaves Bank A for dead!” Same two interest rates, two ways to draw them.

Illustrative rates made for this lesson.

In the advert, Bank B's bar looks about two and a half times as tall as Bank A's. The real rates are 4.2% and 4.5% p.a. On $10,000 saved for a year that is a difference of just $30. Nothing in the advert is false: the trick is where the axis starts. Surveys can do the same thing by choosing who gets asked. A phone poll that only rings landlines in the daytime, or an online survey that never reaches remote communities, can support whatever view its designer wanted. Today you learn to spot both kinds of tilt.

5Worked example — the 3-step method

Bank B's advert from the hook. Is the claim “Our rate leaves Bank A for dead” a fair reading of the data?

1
Read the true values and the axis start
Look at the axis labels before you look at the bars.
Bank A: 4.2%   Bank B: 4.5%
The vertical axis starts at 4.0%, not 0. The axis is truncated.
2
Compare how tall the bars look with the real ratio
Each bar's drawn height starts at the bottom of the axis, 4.0.
Drawn heights: 4.2 − 4.0 = 0.2 and 4.5 − 4.0 = 0.5
Looks like: 0.50.2 = 2.5 times as tall
Really: 4.54.2 ≈ 1.07 times as large
3
Put the difference in context and judge
Ask what the gap is worth in real terms.
Gap: 4.5% − 4.2% = 0.3 percentage points.
On $10,000 for one year: 0.003 × 10,000 = $30 more interest.
VerdictThe numbers are true but the display misleads: the truncated axis turns a 7% difference into a bar 2.5 times as tall. Bank B is better, by $30 a year on $10,000, not “for dead”.

6bSpot the flawed analysis

A student has analysed a report, but one line of their reasoning is wrong. Read each line and click the line where the mistake first appears.

Report: “Only 40% of First Nations adults in the region use the new health clinic. Based on 800 responses to an online survey, written in English, open for one week.” (Illustrative report.)
1The population is all First Nations adults in the region.
2Only people with internet access who read English could answer, so people in remote communities and people who mainly speak an Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander language were largely left out.
3The people left out would probably answer the same way as everyone else, so this does not change the result.
4So about 40% of all First Nations adults in the region use the clinic.

✓ Right: line 3 is where the reasoning breaks.

Why: the people left out are exactly the ones whose experience may be different. Remote residents may rely on the clinic more, or find it harder to reach. Assuming excluded groups “answer the same” hides the bias instead of dealing with it. This is under-coverage, and it can bias a report about First Nations Australians.

Which is the best rewrite of line 3?

  • A: An online survey is always the fairest method, so the result stands.
  • B: The people left out may use the clinic differently, so 40% may not represent all First Nations adults in the region.
  • C: 800 responses is too few to say anything about the region.

★Hinge check: same data, two charts🎯 Pick one

Before the exit ticket: pick the best conclusion. Each wrong option hides a common mistake.

A phone company reports customer satisfaction: Plan X 92%, Plan Y 96%. Chart P draws the bars with the axis starting at 0. Chart Q draws the same bars with the axis starting at 90. What is the best conclusion?
That reads the picture, not the numbers. Chart Q starts at 90, so the bars are drawn 2 and 6 units tall. The real values, 92% and 96%, are nowhere near a ratio of 3.
Correct. 96 − 92 = 4 percentage points. Chart Q truncates the axis at 90, which makes that small gap look huge. Chart P, starting at 0, shows it honestly.
Starting at zero is the honest choice for a bar chart. The bars in Chart P look similar because 92% and 96% really are similar.
Same data, different axis. Both charts show 92% and 96%. Only the starting value of the vertical axis changed.
