1Why this matters

A real situation that is secretly today's maths.

The SRC budgeted $4000 for the school fete. The final bill came to $5000. At the same time, the hall was measured as 18 m long, to the nearest metre, to order bunting.

The budget was out by $1000. Compared with what was really spent, that is 10005000 × 100% = 20%: a big miss for a treasurer. And "18 m to the nearest metre" means the hall could really be anywhere from 17.5 m up to 18.5 m. Today you will judge errors in real money and crowd predictions, and give lower and upper bounds for any measurement.

Actual vs budget
Tap to flip
The actual amount (what was really spent or who really came) is the exact value. The budget or prediction is the estimate.
Budget $4000; actual $5000. Divide by 5000.
Overspend / underspend
Tap to flip
Overspend: actual is more than the budget. Underspend: actual is less than the budget.
Budget $4000, spent $5000: overspend $1000.
Precision
Tap to flip
The smallest unit a measurement is rounded to, e.g. the nearest metre, 0.1 kg or 10 people.
18 m to the nearest metre: precision 1 m.
Maximum error
Tap to flip
Half the precision. The measured value can be out by at most this much.
Nearest metre: maximum error 0.5 m.
Lower and upper bounds
Tap to flip
Lower bound = measured − maximum error. Upper bound = measured + maximum error. The true value lies between them.
18 m → 17.5 m to 18.5 m.

5Worked example — the 3-step method

A rug is measured as 3.6 m long, correct to the nearest 0.1 m. Give the lower and upper bounds for its true length, and decide whether it will definitely fit along a 3.7 m wall.

1
Find the precision, then halve it
"Nearest 0.1 m" means the precision is 0.1 m. Any length that rounds to 3.6 m can be at most half of that away.
Maximum error = 0.1 ÷ 2 = 0.05 m
2
Subtract and add the maximum error
Go down by 0.05 m for the lower bound and up by 0.05 m for the upper bound.
Lower bound = 3.6 − 0.05 = 3.55 m
Upper bound = 3.6 + 0.05 = 3.65 m
3
Interpret in context
Use the bound that matters. To be sure it fits, check the largest possible length.
3.55 m ≤ true length < 3.65 m. Even the upper bound, 3.65 m, is less than 3.7 m.
Trap: using the whole precision (3.5 m to 3.7 m) doubles the error.
Answer Bounds 3.55 m and 3.65 m  ·  the rug will definitely fit

Example 1: fully worked
A hall is 18 m long, to the nearest metre.
Precision = 1 m
Maximum error = 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5 m

★Hinge check 🎯 Pick one

A running track is measured as 25 m long, correct to the nearest metre. Which statement about its true length is correct?

You used the whole precision. A length of 24.3 m would round to 24 m, not 25 m. The maximum error is half of 1 m, so go 0.5 m each way.
Correct. Precision 1 m, so the maximum error is 0.5 m. Lower bound 25 − 0.5 = 24.5 m; upper bound 25 + 0.5 = 25.5 m.
That is the nearest 0.1 m. These bounds would suit a reading of 25.0 m to the nearest tenth. Here the precision is 1 m, so halve 1, not 0.1.
Rounding works both ways. 24.7 m also rounds to 25 m, so the true length can be below 25 m too. Go half a metre down and half a metre up.
