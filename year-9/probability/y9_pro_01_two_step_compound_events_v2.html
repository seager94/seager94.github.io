1Why this matters

A real situation that is secretly today's probability.

Your class runs a Heads and Tails stall at the school fair. A player pays $2, flips two coins, and wins $5 if both land heads.

Someone says: “There are three results: two heads, one head, no heads. So you win one game in three and the stall loses money.” Is that right? Before you set the prices you need to know the real chance of two heads.

Flipping two coins is a compound event: two steps, one after the other. Today you list every outcome with a systematic list or an array, then find a probability by counting: favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes.

3Two coins: which probability?

Sort each two-coin event by its probability
Two heads Exactly one head Two tails First coin is heads Head first, then tail (HT) Both coins land the same Tail first, then head (TH) Second coin is tails

P = ¼ (1 outcome of 4)

P = ½ (2 outcomes of 4)

💡 HT and TH are different outcomes: the first coin is different. That is why “exactly one head” is twice as likely as “two heads”.

5Worked example — the 3-step method

Heads and Tails: two fair coins are flipped. Find the probability of getting exactly one head.

1
List every outcome systematically
Put the first coin down the side and the second coin across the top. Each cell is one outcome.
Sample space = {HH, HT, TH, TT}: 4 equally likely outcomes
2
Count the favourable outcomes
Exactly one head happens in HT and in TH: they are different outcomes.
Favourable outcomes = 2
3
Divide and check
P(event) = favourable ÷ total. Check: the four probabilities in the array add to 1.
P(exactly one head) = 24 = 12

6bSpot the mistake

A student has answered a Heads and Tails question, but one line of their working is wrong. Click the line where the mistake first appears.

Two fair coins are flipped. Find P(exactly one head).
1Count how many heads show on the two coins.
2The number of heads can be 0, 1 or 2.
3So there are 3 equally likely outcomes: no heads, one head, two heads.
4P(exactly one head) = 1 out of 3 = 13

✓ Right: line 3 is where the reasoning breaks.

Why: 0, 1 and 2 heads are possible results, but they are not equally likely. List the outcomes coin by coin: HH, HT, TH, TT. “One head” happens two ways (HT and TH), so it is twice as likely as “two heads”.

Which is the best rewrite of line 3?

  • A: The equally likely outcomes are HH, HT, TH and TT, so there are 4 outcomes.
  • B: The 3 results are not equally likely, so the probability cannot be found.
  • C: There are only 2 outcomes, heads or tails.

7bHinge check: two dice🎯 Pick one

Before you pick your tier: choose the correct answer. Each wrong option hides a common mistake.

Two fair dice are rolled and the numbers are added. What is P(the sum is 4)?
The sums are not equally likely. A sum of 7 can be made six ways but a sum of 2 only one way. Count cells in the 36-cell array, not possible sums.
Order matters for the outcomes. A 1 on the first die and a 3 on the second is a different cell from a 3 then a 1. You missed (3, 1).
Correct. Three cells of the 6 × 6 array give a sum of 4, so P = 336 = 112.
Multiply, don't add. Each of the 6 results on the first die pairs with each of the 6 on the second: 6 × 6 = 36 outcomes, not 6 + 6 = 12.
