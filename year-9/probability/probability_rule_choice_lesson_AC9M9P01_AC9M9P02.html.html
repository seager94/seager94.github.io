1Warm-up -- pick your tool

Today's lesson isn't about new formulas. It's about choosing the right one -- fast.

You already know the four probability rules:

  • Multiplication (AND) -- for compound events
  • Addition (OR) -- for either-or events
  • Complement -- for "at least one" shortcuts
  • Conditional -- P(B given A)

Today's skill is recognising which one a problem is asking for, before you start calculating. A tradie doesn't pick a hammer for every job -- and neither should you.

Rapid-fire: I'll call out a scenario, you call out the rule. We're aiming for the decision in under 5 seconds -- calculation can wait.

Then we'll learn a 4-step routine that turns rule-choice from a guess into a habit.

💡 Watch out for "heart OR a king". It's still an OR, but they're not mutually exclusive -- the king of hearts is both. We'll need P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).

4Always, sometimes, or never?

Drag each claim into the right bucket. The "sometimes" bucket is where the careful thinking lives -- what extra condition makes it true?

P(A) + P(not A) = 1 P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) Mutually exclusive events are also independent For independent events, P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B) Using the complement is faster than direct calculation P(A ∩ B) > P(A) P(A ∪ B) ≥ P(A) P(B | A) = P(B) Drawing two cards without replacement gives independent events

✓ Always true

~ Sometimes true

✗ Never true

💡 The big one: "mutually exclusive" and "independent" feel similar but they're opposites. If A and B are mutually exclusive (can't both happen), then knowing A happened tells you B didn't -- that's maximally dependent.
The sometimes claims all hide a condition: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) only when mutually exclusive; P(B | A) = P(B) only when independent; the complement only saves time when "none of them" is easier to compute than the direct case.

5Worked example -- two cards without replacement

Two cards are drawn from a standard 52-card deck without replacement. Find P(1st card is an ace ∩ 2nd card is a king).

Step 0 -- Make the decision first

1What's asked? AND -- intersection of two events.
2Independent or dependent? Dependent -- without replacement, the deck changes.
3Mutually exclusive? No -- both events can happen on the same pair of draws.
4Any shortcut? No -- straight multiplication with conditional probability.
1
Identify the values
P(1st ace) is straightforward. For P(2nd king | 1st ace), the deck now has 51 cards and still 4 kings.
P(1st ace) = 452
P(2nd king | 1st ace) = 451
2
Apply the rule -- dependent multiplication
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A). Multiply the two probabilities.
P(1st ace ∩ 2nd king) = 452 × 451
= 162652 = 4663
3
Verify and interpret
Is the answer sensible? About 4663 ≈ 0.006, so roughly 6 in a thousand draws. That feels right -- we're asking for a specific pair in a specific order from 52 cards.
Check: numerator 4 × 4 = 16 specific outcomes; denominator 52 × 51 = 2652 ordered pairs. Ratio = 162652 = 4663. ✓
Answer P(1st ace ∩ 2nd king) = 4663
