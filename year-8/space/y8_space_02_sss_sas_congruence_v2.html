1Warm-up — do we always need all six measurements?

Last lesson you learnt that congruent triangles have all six pairs of corresponding parts equal — three sides and three angles. Today's question: do you really need to check all six?

Try this in your head 🍝

Imagine I give you three uncooked spaghetti pieces of fixed lengths — say 5 cm, 7 cm and 8 cm. You snap their ends together to form a triangle.

How many different shapes of triangle can you make? Only one. The three lengths force the triangle to take a single shape.

Now imagine I give you just two spaghetti pieces — 5 cm and 7 cm — and pin them at one end so the angle between them is exactly 40°. Again, only one triangle shape is possible.

In both cases, three pieces of information were enough to lock down the triangle. That's the big idea today.

🎯 Today's framing question
"Do we always need to check all six measurements (3 sides, 3 angles) to prove two triangles are congruent?"
Short answer: no. Today we meet two shortcuts — SSS and SAS — that prove congruence from just three pieces of information.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. The phrase included angle is the make-or-break term today.

Congruence test
Tap to flip
A shortcut rule — a small set of equal parts that guarantees two triangles are congruent, without checking all six.
SSS and SAS are two such tests. ASA and RHS come next lesson.
Included angle
Tap to flip
The angle between two named sides — the angle at the vertex where the two sides meet.
In △ABC, the angle included between AB and BC is ∠B (at the shared vertex).
Non-included angle
Tap to flip
An angle that is NOT between the two named sides — it sits at one of the other vertices.
In △ABC, ∠A is not included between AB and BC (the shared vertex is B, not A).
SSS test
Tap to flip
Side–Side–Side. If all three pairs of corresponding sides are equal, the triangles are congruent.
AB=DE, BC=EF, CA=FD ⟹ △ABC ≅ △DEF (SSS)
SAS test
Tap to flip
Side–Angle–Side. If two pairs of sides are equal AND the included angles are equal, the triangles are congruent.
AB=DE, ∠B=∠E, BC=EF ⟹ △ABC ≅ △DEF (SAS)

6Together — SAS: triangles with two sides and the included angle

Same routine as the I Do, but this time we have only two pairs of sides. We'll need an angle to fill the gap — and the angle has to be in the right place.

GIVEN: In △PQR and △XYZ, PQ = XY = 6 cm, QR = YZ = 9 cm, and ∠Q = ∠Y = 40°.
SHOW: △PQR ≅ △XYZ, naming the test.
△PQR
≅?
△XYZ
💡 Why this matters: the 40° angle sits at Q, the vertex where PQ and QR meet. That's why we call it the included angle — and it's why SAS works. If the angle had been at P or R instead, we couldn't use SAS.
⚠️ Misconception alert (the SSA trap): if you're given two sides AND an angle that is not between them, you cannot use SAS — and there is no congruence test that fits this pattern. Two genuinely different triangles can have the same two sides and a non-included angle. We'll meet this "ambiguous case" next lesson.
