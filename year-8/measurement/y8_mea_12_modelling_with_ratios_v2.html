1Why this matters

A real situation where ratio modelling is the only way to get the right answer.

An architect draws a floor plan at a scale of 1 : 200. On the drawing, the lounge room measures 3.5 cm × 2.8 cm. Before the builder orders timber flooring, they need to know the actual dimensions — and the area in square metres to price the job.

Without a reliable model connecting the drawing to reality, the builder guesses — and the timber order could be completely wrong. The same challenge appears when a scientist uses a microscope (magnifying a ratio the other way), or a chef doubles a recipe without ruining the balance of flavours.

In every case, the mathematics is identical: set up a ratio, solve it, then check your answer makes sense in the real world. That cycle — formulate, model, solve, interpret, review — is what this lesson is about.

Three contexts for ratio modelling. The same cycle applies to all three.

Scale ratio
Tap to flip
A ratio comparing a drawn length to the real length it represents. Written as drawing : real.
Scale 1 : 200 means 1 cm on paper = 200 cm (2 m) in reality.
Scale factor
Tap to flip
The number you multiply by to move from one quantity to the other in a ratio relationship.
Scale 1 : 200 → multiply drawing measurement by 200 to find real length.
Magnification
Tap to flip
How many times larger an image is than the real object. A ratio of image size to actual size.
Image 2.4 cm, real 0.006 cm → magnification = 400×.

Tap to flip

A room of 49 000 m² is clearly wrong for a house; the correct answer is 49 m².

5Worked example — floor plan scale

A bedroom on a floor plan measures 2.5 cm × 2.0 cm. The scale is 1 : 200. Find the real dimensions and the actual area of the bedroom.

1
Formulate — identify what we know and what we need
Read the problem and decide what the model needs to do.
Scale = 1 : 200 → 1 unit on drawing = 200 units in reality
Drawing dimensions: 2.5 cm (width) and 2.0 cm (length)
Question: Find real width, real length, then real area.
2
Model & Solve — apply the scale factor
Multiply each drawing measurement by the scale factor (200). Then convert to metres.
Real width = 2.5 cm × 200 = 500 cm = 5.0 m
Real length = 2.0 cm × 200 = 400 cm = 4.0 m
Real area = 5.0 m × 4.0 m = 20.0 m²
3
Interpret & Review — state the answer, check it's reasonable
Give the answer in context and sense-check against reality.
The bedroom is 5.0 m wide and 4.0 m long, with an area of 20.0 m².

Review: A typical bedroom in Australia is 10–20 m². 20 m² is on the large side but realistic for a master bedroom. ✓
Also: 500 cm ÷ 200 = 2.5 cm, which matches the drawing. ✓
Modelling answer Bedroom: 5.0 m × 4.0 m, area = 20.0 m²

5 (continued) Worked example — scaling a recipe

A pancake recipe serves 4 and uses 300 g flour, 2 eggs, 150 mL milk, and 60 g butter. Scale it up for a school breakfast of 30 people, then check whether the model is reasonable.

1
Formulate — identify the ratio relationship
The recipe ratio is: each ingredient is in a fixed ratio to the number of serves.
Original serves = 4.   Target serves = 30.
Scale factor = 30 ÷ 4 = 7.5
Every ingredient amount must be multiplied by 7.5 to keep the ratios constant.
2
Model & Solve — scale each ingredient
Multiply every ingredient by the scale factor. The ratios between ingredients stay constant.
Flour: 300 × 7.5 = 2 250 g
Eggs: 2 × 7.5 = 15 eggs
Milk: 150 × 7.5 = 1 125 mL
Butter: 60 × 7.5 = 450 g
3
Interpret & Review — check for reasonableness and practicality
State the answer in context. Then review: are these amounts sensible for a school breakfast? Are there practical constraints the model ignores?
For 30 people: 2 250 g flour, 15 eggs, 1 125 mL milk, 450 g butter.

Review — model limitations:
• 15 eggs is a whole number ✓ (7.5 × 2 = 15 exactly — no rounding needed)
• 1 125 mL ≈ just over 1 litre — sensible ✓
• The model assumes the ratio of ingredients must be kept exactly. In practice, a cook might round 15 eggs to 15 (exact here), or round flour to 2 kg for ease.
• The model does not account for cooking time (pans, batches) — that is outside the mathematical model.
Modelling answer — reviewed Flour 2 250 g · Eggs 15 · Milk 1 125 mL · Butter 450 g. Model is reasonable; practical rounding of flour to 2.25 kg is acceptable.

Things you could have found
Real dimensions of the Reading Room
Drawing: 6 cm × 4 cm. Scale factor = 250.
Real width = 6 × 250 = 1 500 cm = 15 m
Real depth = 4 × 250 = 1 000 cm = 10 m
Review: 15 m × 10 m is a large room — reasonable for a school library reading room.
Real area of the Reading Room
Real area = 15 m × 10 m = 150 m²
Review: A school library reading room of 150 m² fits comfortably — a typical classroom is about 56 m², so this is around 2.5 classrooms in size. Reasonable.
Real dimensions and area of the Computer Lab
Drawing: 3 cm × 2 cm.
Real: 3 × 250 = 750 cm = 7.5 m; 2 × 250 = 500 cm = 5 m
Area = 7.5 × 5 = 37.5 m²
Review: 37.5 m² is slightly smaller than a classroom — fine for a computer lab.
Real dimensions and area of the Storage room
Drawing: 3 cm × 3 cm.
Real: 3 × 250 = 750 cm = 7.5 m; 3 × 250 = 750 cm = 7.5 m
Area = 7.5 × 7.5 = 56.25 m²
Review: 56 m² storage seems very large — worth questioning whether this is a realistic storage allocation or whether part of this space might be better used otherwise.
Real dimensions and area of the Corridor
Drawing: 9 cm × 1 cm.
Real: 9 × 250 = 2 250 cm = 22.5 m; 1 × 250 = 250 cm = 2.5 m
Area = 22.5 × 2.5 = 56.25 m²
Review: 2.5 m wide is a minimum corridor width for wheelchair access. Reasonable.
Total floor area of the extension
Total drawing area = (9 cm × 5 cm) = 45 cm² — or sum the rooms: 24 + 6 + 9 + 9 = 48 cm²
Alternatively, using real areas: 150 + 37.5 + 56.25 + 56.25 = 300 m²
Review: 300 m² for a library extension — comparable to a large school building wing. Sensible.
Working backwards — what would the Drawing Room measure if the real room were 12 m × 8 m at this scale?
Drawing width = 1 200 cm ÷ 250 = 4.8 cm; Drawing depth = 800 ÷ 250 = 3.2 cm
This reverses the model: going from real → drawing by dividing by the scale factor.
