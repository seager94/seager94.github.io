1Why this matters

A real situation that’s secretly today’s maths.

Each summer, Burnside City Council decides which suburban parks to spray for mosquitoes. The catch: they pay the contractor per square metre. A park shaped like an L or a house pentagon doesn’t come with a ready-made formula on the invoice.

The council officer sketches the park on 1 cm grid paper, breaks it into rectangles and triangles she knows how to handle, adds the pieces together — and gets a quoted cost within minutes. For oddly-shaped wetland areas she simply counts squares on the map.

Today you’ll learn the same two strategies: decompose-and-add (or enclose-and-subtract) for composite shapes, and grid-counting for truly irregular regions.

The big idea: any composite shape can be thought of as two (or more) simple shapes glued together, or equally as a big shape with a piece removed. Both views give the same area — which you’ll see side by side later in the lesson.

For curves and blobs, a grid gives you an estimate that’s good enough for practical decisions like spray coverage.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. These five terms are the language of today’s lesson.

Composite shape
Tap to flip
A shape made by joining two or more simple shapes (rectangles, triangles, etc.).
An L-shape = two rectangles joined together.
Decompose
Tap to flip
Split a shape into simpler pieces so you can find each area separately, then add.
Cut an L-shape along an internal line to make two rectangles.
Bounding rectangle
Tap to flip
The smallest rectangle that completely encloses a shape. Used in the enclose-and-subtract method.
Draw a big rectangle around an L-shape, then subtract the missing corner.
Irregular shape
Tap to flip
A shape with no formula because the sides are curved or uneven. We estimate its area using a grid.
A lake outline, a puddle, a wetland zone on a map.
Grid-square method
Tap to flip
Count full squares inside the shape, then count part squares (typically ½ each). Multiply count by the area of one square.
On a 1 cm grid: 14 full squares + 6 half-squares → 14 + 3 = 17 cm².

5Worked example A — L-shape by decompose-and-add

Find the area of this L-shape. The dashed line shows where we split it into two rectangles.

1
Identify the two rectangles
The dashed line cuts the L into two rectangles. Read off the dimensions of each.
Rectangle A: 6 m × 4 m
Rectangle B: 10 m × 5 m
2
Find each area
Area of a rectangle = length × width.
A = 6 × 4 = 24 m²
B = 10 × 5 = 50 m²
3
Add the pieces
Total area = A + B.
24 + 50 = 74 m²
Answer (Add method) Area = 74 m²

5bWorked example B — same L-shape by enclose-and-subtract

Same shape, same answer — but this time we enclose it in a big rectangle and subtract the missing corner.

1
Draw the bounding rectangle
Wrap the whole L in the smallest rectangle that fits around it.
Bounding rectangle: 16 m × 10 m
Areabig = 16 × 10 = 160 m²
2
Find the cut-out corner
The missing top-right corner is a rectangle too.
Cut-out: 10 m × 4 m
Areacut = 10 × 4 = 86 m²
3
Subtract
Area of L-shape = big − cut-out.
160 − 86 = 74 m²
Answer (Subtract method) Area = 74 m²  ✓ Same answer!

5cWorked example C — house pentagon (rectangle + triangle)

A council shed has a house-shaped cross-section. Find its area.

1
Split into rectangle and triangle
The dashed line separates the rectangular walls from the triangular roof.
Rectangle: 12 m × 5 m
Triangle: base 12 m, height 6 m
2
Find each area
Use A = l × w for the rectangle, A = ½ × b × h for the triangle.
Rectangle = 12 × 5 = 60 m²
Triangle = ½ × 12 × 6 = 36 m²
3
Add the areas
60 + 36 = 96 m²
Answer Area of house cross-section = 96 m²

5dWorked example D — irregular wetland area (grid counting)

Council needs to estimate the area of a wetland breeding zone for mosquitoes. The region is plotted on a 1 cm grid where each square represents 4 m².

1
Count the full squares
Mark every square completely inside the blob. Count carefully.
Full squares inside blob: 14
2
Count the part squares
Mark squares that are partly inside. Count each as ½ (it’s an estimate).
Part squares: 7 × ½ = 3.5 squares
3
Multiply by the area of one square
Total squares × area per square gives the estimated real-world area.
Total squares = 14 + 3.5 = 17.5
Area ≈ 17.5 × 4 m² = 70 m²
Answer Wetland area ≈ 70 m²  (estimate)
Why is it an estimate? Part squares that are a tiny sliver count the same as part squares that are nearly full. The more accurate rule is to only count part squares that are more than half full — but for Year 8 we use the ½ convention unless told otherwise.

6Compare two solutions — same shape, two methods

Below is a U-shaped composite shape. Both solutions give the same area. Study each method, then answer the questions at the bottom.

Method 1 Decompose & add

Split the U into three rectangles: bottom bar + left column + right column.

Bottom bar: 14 × 4 = 56 m²
Left column: 3 × 6 = 18 m²
Right column: 3 × 6 = 18 m²
Total = 56 + 18 + 18 = 92 m²

Method 2 Enclose & subtract

Wrap the U in a 14×10 bounding rectangle, then subtract the inner cut-out.

Bounding rectangle: 14 × 10 = 140 m²
Cut-out (inner gap): 8 × 6 = 48 m²
 
Total = 140 − 48 = 92 m²
Class discussion — answer together:
  1. What is the same about the two methods?
  2. What is different about the two methods?
  3. Method 1 needed three rectangles. Method 2 needed only one subtraction. When is Method 2 (subtract) more efficient?
  4. Could you use Method 2 on the house-pentagon example from worked example C? Why or why not?
