1Why this matters

A real situation where perimeter of a composite shape is exactly what you need.

A builder is quoting to install skirting boards around an L-shaped living room. Skirting board costs $12 per metre. She needs to know the total distance around the room's walls — not the floor area, just the boundary.

The room isn't a simple rectangle: it has an internal notch where a hallway joins. Some wall lengths are marked on the plan; others have to be worked out from the ones that are given.

Getting the perimeter wrong by even half a metre means ordering too little material — a costly re-delivery fee on top.

Today's skill: find the perimeter of composite shapes — shapes made by joining or cutting rectangles — by
  1. labelling every side of the boundary,
  2. calculating any unlabelled sides from the ones you know,
  3. adding every boundary side together.
Perimeter = total distance around the outside only.

Composite shape

A shape made by joining or cutting two or more simpler shapes.
An L-shape is two rectangles joined together.
Boundary
Tap to flip
The outer edge of a shape — only the sides you can walk around.
Internal edges where two rectangles join are not part of the boundary.
Unlabelled side
Tap to flip
A boundary side whose length is not given directly — you calculate it from opposite sides.
If the total width is 9 m and one part is 4 m, the unlabelled part is 5 m.

Key idea: when you decompose a composite shape into rectangles, the lines you draw to split it are not part of the perimeter — they're internal construction lines. Only count sides that form the outer boundary of the whole shape.

5Worked example 1 — L-shaped room

Find the perimeter. One side is unlabelled — work it out first, then sum every boundary edge.

L-shaped room. All measurements in metres. The top-left side (shown in terracotta) must be found before summing.
Common error to avoid: students often forget to find the unlabelled side and simply add only the given numbers. Every boundary side must appear in your sum — count the sides on the diagram before you start.
1
Count and label every boundary side
Walk around the outside of the L-shape. There are 6 boundary sides. Five are given; one (the top-left) is marked with a question mark.
Given sides: 9 m, 8 m, 4 m, 3 m, 5 m
Unknown side: ? m (top-left edge)
2
Find the unlabelled side
The top-left edge runs across the same direction as the bottom (9 m). The top-right step uses 4 m of that width. So the unlabelled side = total width − step width.
? = 9 − 4 = 5 m
3
Add every boundary side
Now all 6 sides are known. Sum them to get the perimeter.
P = 9 + 8 + 5 + 3 + 4 + 5
P = 34 m
Answer Perimeter = 34 m

6Worked example 2 — T-shaped garden bed

This time two sides are unlabelled. Find each one before summing.

T-shaped garden bed. Two sides (shown in terracotta) are unlabelled — both must be found before summing.
1
Count and label every boundary side
The T-shape has 8 boundary sides. Six are given. Two "shoulder" sides (horizontal, at the base of the top bar) are marked with ?.
Given: top = 12 m, both bar-side heights = 3 m each, both stem sides = 6 m each, stem bottom = 5 m
Unknown: right shoulder = ? and left shoulder = ?
2
Find both unlabelled sides
The top bar is 12 m wide. The stem is 5 m wide. The two shoulders share the remaining width: 12 − 5 = 7 m total shoulder. Right shoulder = 4 m (given by the asymmetric diagram), so left shoulder = 7 − 4 = 3 m.
Total top width = 12 m, stem = 5 m
Total shoulder = 12 − 5 = 7 m
Right shoulder = 4 m (from diagram position)
Left shoulder = 7 − 4 = 3 m
3
Add every boundary side
All 8 sides now known. Sum them.
P = 12 + 3 + 4 + 6 + 5 + 6 + 3 + 3
P = 42 m
Answer Perimeter = 42 m

7Together — rectangle with a rectangular notch

A 10 m × 7 m rectangle has a 3 m × 2 m notch cut from its top-right corner. Find the perimeter step by step.

10 m × 7 m rectangle with 3 m × 2 m notch cut from top-right. The two terracotta sides must be found from the given measurements.
Key insight: cutting a notch from a corner does not change the perimeter by as much as you might think — the two sides of the notch (2 m and 3 m) replace a corner, but you also lose two parts of the original boundary. In this case the perimeter is actually the same as the full rectangle: 10 + 7 + 10 + 7 = 34 m. Can you see why? The notch adds 2 + 3 = 5 m but removes 5 m of corner — net change is zero!
