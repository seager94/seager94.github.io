1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's algebra.

A garden designer is tiling two adjoining sections of a courtyard. The first section needs 6x tiles and the second needs 9 tiles — a total of 6x + 9 tiles.

Tiles are sold in packs of 3. How many packs are needed? The designer needs to see whether every part of the order is divisible by 3.

Factorising reveals the answer:

6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3)
Exactly 3 packs, each covering (2x + 3) tiles. Factorising is the reverse of expanding — it applies the distributive property backwards to reveal hidden structure.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. You'll use these terms all lesson.

Factor
Tap to flip
A number or expression that divides exactly into another with no remainder.
Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. Factors of 6x: 1, 2, 3, 6, x, 2x, 3x, 6x.
HCF
Tap to flip
The Highest Common Factor — the largest factor shared by two or more terms.
HCF of 6x and 9: factors of 6 are 1, 2, 3, 6 and of 9 are 1, 3, 9, so HCF = 3.
Factorise
Tap to flip
To rewrite an expression as a product of its factors. The reverse of expanding.
Factorising 2x + 4 gives 2(x + 2) — the HCF sits outside.
Distributive Property (in reverse)
Tap to flip
Expanding reads left to right: a(b + c) = ab + ac. Factorising reads right to left:
ab + ac = a(b + c)
Same property, opposite direction.
Equivalent Expressions
Tap to flip
Two expressions that give the same value for every value of the variable.
2x + 4 and 2(x + 2) are equivalent — check by expanding the factorised form.

5Worked examples — the 3-step factorising method

Three examples showing the method. Work through each one before moving on.

Example A  Factorise 2x + 4

1
Find the HCF of all terms
List the numerical factors of each term's coefficient. The largest shared factor is the HCF — it will sit outside the bracket.
Terms: 2x and +4

Factors of 2:  1, 2
Factors of 4:  1, 2, 4

HCF = 2  ← this goes outside the bracket
2
Use the reverse area model — divide each term by the HCF
Write the HCF as the side of a rectangle. The cell contents (the original terms) tell you what goes across the top — inside the bracket.
x  (= 2x ÷ 2) + 2  (= 4 ÷ 2)
2 2x 4
The top row reveals what goes inside the bracket.
3
Write the factorised form and verify by re-expanding
Join the HCF and the bracket. Then expand to confirm you get the original expression back.
Factorised: 2(x + 2)

Verify: 2(x + 2) = 2 × x + 2 × 2 = 2x + 4  ✓
Distributive property — in reverse 2x + 4 = 2(x + 2)

Example B  Factorise 6a − 9

1
Find the HCF of 6 and 9
List factors of each numerical coefficient. The sign on a term does not affect which factors it has — only the number matters.
Terms: 6a and −9

Factors of 6:  1, 2, 3, 6
Factors of 9:  1, 3, 9

Common factors: 1, 3  →  HCF = 3
2
Divide each term by the HCF — keep the sign
The sign belongs to the term, not the bracket. When you divide, the sign travels inside with the result.
6a ÷ 3 = 2a   →  goes inside the bracket
−9 ÷ 3 = −3   →  sign travels inside too

Bracket contents: 2a − 3
Write: 3(2a − 3)
3
Verify by re-expanding
Apply the distributive property to 3(2a − 3). If you recover 6a − 9, the factorisation is correct.
3(2a − 3) = 3 × 2a + 3 × (−3)
= 6a − 9  ✓  (matches the original)
Distributive property — in reverse 6a − 9 = 3(2a − 3)

Example C  Factorise 12p + 8  — finding a less obvious HCF

1
List all factors and identify the HCF
When coefficients are larger, list all factors carefully. The HCF must be the highest common factor — choosing a smaller common factor gives an incomplete factorisation.
Terms: 12p and +8

Factors of 12:  1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
Factors of 8:  1, 2, 4, 8

Common factors: 1, 2, 4  →  HCF = 4  (choose the largest!)
2
Divide each term by 4
Calculate each quotient precisely. The results go inside the bracket.
12p ÷ 4 = 3p
8 ÷ 4  = 2

Write: 4(3p + 2)
3
Verify by re-expanding
Expand 4(3p + 2) and check it matches 12p + 8.
4(3p + 2) = 4 × 3p + 4 × 2
= 12p + 8  ✓
Key point — always use the highest common factor 12p + 8 = 4(3p + 2)  (using HCF = 4, not 2)
