1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's statistics.

Your school is deciding on a new uniform proposal. Two class captains each ask a random sample of 20 students the same question: "Are you in favour?" One captain reports 60% in favour; the other reports 45%.

Neither miscounted. They asked different random samples, so they got different proportions. If each had asked 200 students instead of 20, the two answers would have landed much closer together.

That gap between random samples is sampling variation. It never disappears, but it shrinks as the sample gets bigger. Today we'll draw samples of two different sizes from the same population and watch the larger samples settle down — the exact skill scientists rely on, from opinion polls to First Nations Ranger biodiversity surveys.

4Worked example — predict from relative frequency

Over the last three wet seasons, Rangers recorded a native sedge in 156 of their 240 survey quadrats. This season they plan 500 quadrats. About how many will contain the sedge?

1
Find the relative frequency from the historical data
Relative frequency = number of times it happened ÷ number of quadrats surveyed.
Relative frequency = 156240 = 0.65
2
Read it as an estimated proportion
Because it comes from many past quadrats, 0.65 is our best estimate of the proportion of quadrats containing the sedge — about 65%.
Estimated proportion = 0.65 = 65%
3
Apply the proportion to this season's plan
Multiply the estimate by the new number of quadrats. It's a prediction, not a guarantee — the real count will vary a little (sampling variation), and the large history makes this estimate more trustworthy.
Predicted quadrats = 0.65 × 500 = 325
Prediction Relative frequency 0.65 → about 325 of 500 quadrats

7Always, sometimes, or never?

Drag each claim about samples into the bucket where it belongs. Wrong drops shake and bounce back — that's the auto-marking. Then press Show answers & reasons to reveal the justification for each. The "sometimes" bucket is where the careful thinking lives.

In a random sample, every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen A sample proportion must be between 0% and 100% A larger random sample tends to vary less from sample to sample than a smaller one A larger sample gives a mean closer to the population mean Two random samples of the same size give the same median A sample of 30 is large enough to represent the population well A single random sample tells you the exact population mean with certainty Sampling variation disappears completely once the sample is large enough Two different random samples must give exactly the same proportion

✓ Always true

~ Sometimes true

✗ Never true

AlwaysEvery member has an equal chance of being chosen. That equal chance is the very definition of a random sample.
AlwaysA proportion is between 0% and 100%. A proportion is a part out of a whole, so it can't be negative or exceed 100%.
AlwaysA larger random sample tends to vary less. As a general tendency, increasing the sample size reduces the sample-to-sample variation.
SometimesA larger sample gives a mean closer to the population mean. Larger samples are more likely to be close, but any single large sample can still land further out than a lucky small one.
SometimesTwo random samples of the same size give the same median. They might coincide, but usually different samples give different medians.
SometimesA sample of 30 represents the population well. It depends on how varied the population is and what question is being asked — 30 may be plenty for one population and too few for another.
NeverA single sample gives the exact population mean with certainty. A sample only estimates the population; some sampling variation always remains.
NeverSampling variation disappears completely. Variation shrinks as n grows but never reaches zero unless you measure the entire population.
NeverTwo different random samples must give exactly the same proportion. Different random samples almost always differ — they are not required to match.
💡 Notice the two required claims both sit in "sometimes": a bigger sample is only more likely to be closer, and two same-size samples only occasionally share a median. "More likely" is not "always".
