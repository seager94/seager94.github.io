1Why this matters

A real investigation that is secretly today’s statistics.

During the 2020 pandemic lockdowns, many people stayed home all day. Energy companies wanted to know: did households use more electricity than before?

There are hundreds of thousands of households in South Australia. Measuring every single one would cost too much and take too long, so researchers studied a carefully chosen sample instead.

From a sample of just a few hundred households, statisticians estimated the change for the whole population — then reported it honestly as an estimate with uncertainty, not an exact fact. Today you will plan and run your own investigation the same way: pose a question → design a sample → collect → analyse → report.

Sample
Tap to flip
A smaller group chosen from the population that you actually collect data from.
Example: 500 households chosen from all SA households.
Random sample
Tap to flip
A sample where every member has an equal chance of being chosen — this helps avoid bias.
Example: numbering every student and drawing names at random.
Inference
Tap to flip
A conclusion about the whole population drawn from sample data — it always carries some uncertainty.
Example: “use rose by about 4 kWh/day” estimated from a sample.

5Worked example — the 3-step method

Investigation: Did households use more electricity during the 2020 lockdown than in 2019?

1
Pose the question & design the sample
The population is all SA households. Measuring every one is impractical, so we take a random sample of 10 households and record the increase in daily electricity use (kWh) from 2019 to 2020. Choosing them randomly keeps the sample fair and representative.
Population: all SA households  •  Sample: 10 randomly chosen households  •  Variable: increase in daily use (kWh)
2
Collect & organise the data
Here is the increase in daily use (kWh) for the 10 sampled households, sorted smallest to largest:
2,  3,  3,  4,  4,  4,  5,  5,  6,  8
3
Analyse with summary statistics, then report
Plot the data and summarise it. Mean = 44 ÷ 10 = 4.4 kWh; median = (4 + 4) ÷ 2 = 4 kWh; mode = 4 kWh; range = 8 − 2 = 6 kWh.
Report (acknowledging uncertainty)On average, sampled households increased daily use by about 4.4 kWh. Because this comes from a sample of only 10, the true population figure may differ — a larger sample would make the estimate more reliable.

6We do — a reconciliation attitudes survey

Reconciliation Australia runs a national survey, the Australian Reconciliation Barometer, to measure progress in the relationship between First Nations (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander) peoples and non-Indigenous Australians. A school cannot survey all 900 of its students, so it takes a random sample of 8 students and asks each to rate, from 1 to 10, how well they understand the histories and cultures of First Nations Australians. Scenario 1 — Did SA households use more electricity during the 2020 lockdown?
  • Record each sampled household’s daily electricity use for 2019 and 2020.
  • Ask: did households use more electricity during the 2020 lockdown than in 2019?
  • Conclude that use rose by about 4 kWh/day on average, noting the estimate carries uncertainty.
  • Randomly select 500 households from all SA households to keep the study manageable.
  • Find the mean increase and draw a dot plot of the differences.
Scenario 2 — Testing a factory’s light globes (a census would destroy the stock).
  • Because testing every globe destroys it, randomly select 60 globes to test.
  • Calculate the mean and median burn-time of the 60 sampled globes.
  • Ask: what is the mean burn-time of the globes this factory makes?
  • Estimate the mean burn-time for all globes, acknowledging the sample may not be exact.
  • Burn each of the 60 sampled globes and record how many hours each one lasts.
Scenario 3 — Has students’ understanding of First Nations histories and cultures grown over the year?
  • Compare the mean survey scores and the spread between the start and end of the year.
  • Randomly select 50 students from across all year levels so the sample is representative.
  • Report whether understanding appears to have improved, acknowledging the uncertainty of a sample.
  • Ask: has students’ understanding of First Nations histories and cultures grown over the year?
  • Survey the same 50 students with the same questions at the start and end of the year.
