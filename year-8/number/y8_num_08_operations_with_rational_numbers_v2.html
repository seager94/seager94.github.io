1Why this matters

A real situation that's secretly today's maths.

Priya runs a small market stall. At the end of a trading day she records these entries against her $100 target:

  • Opening balance: −$3.75 (overdrawn)
  • Refund received: +$112
  • Bank fee charged: −34 of a dollar
  • Interest on loan: −1.5% of $12.50

To find her final balance, Priya needs to combine positive and negative fractions and decimals — and choose the most efficient form at each step.

What we build today:

1. Addition and subtraction — combine positive and negative fractions and decimals, using commutative and associative properties to spot efficient groupings.

2. Multiplication and division — apply sign rules to fractions and decimals; convert between forms to enable cross-cancellation.

3. Flexible form choice — decide whether a fraction or decimal is more efficient for each step, then switch if needed.

2Key vocabulary

Tap each card to flip and reveal the definition. These five terms are the language of today's lesson.

Rational number
Tap to flip
Any number that can be written as pq where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0.
All fractions and terminating or recurring decimals are rational. −¾, 2.5, −0.333… are all rational.
Reciprocal
Tap to flip
The multiplicative inverse of a number — found by flipping the fraction. Multiplying a number by its reciprocal gives 1.
The reciprocal of 34 is 43. Dividing by 34 means multiplying by 43.
Commutative property
Tap to flip
For addition and multiplication, order doesn't change the result: a + b = b + a and a × b = b × a.
−¾ + 1.5 + ¾ = −¾ + ¾ + 1.5 = 1.5. Reordering lets the fractions cancel first.
Associative property
Tap to flip
For addition and multiplication, regrouping doesn't change the result: (a + b) + c = a + (b + c).
(−1.8 + ¾) + 1.8 = −1.8 + (¾ + 1.8). Regrouping lets us pair −1.8 and 1.8 to cancel.
Efficient strategy
Tap to flip
Choosing the form or grouping that makes calculation simpler — fewer steps, less chance of error.
Converting 2.5 to 52 before multiplying by 45 enables cancellation → faster and exact.

Example 1 — Regrouping for efficient addition/subtraction: calculate −1.8 + (−¾) + 1.8 + (−2¼)
1
Spot efficient pairs — use commutativity to reorder
Look for decimals that cancel each other, and fractions that share a denominator. Reorder the terms — addition is commutative, so the order doesn't matter.
−1.8 + (−¾) + 1.8 + (−2¼)
↓ reorder (commutativity): place cancelling decimals together, fractions together
= (−1.8 + 1.8) + (−¾ + (−2¼))
    ↑ these cancel to 0        ↑ both have denominator 4
2
Evaluate each group separately
The cancelling decimals give 0. Convert −2¼ to quarters: −2¼ = −94. Then add the fractions over the common denominator 4.
(−1.8 + 1.8) = 0
(−¾ + (−2¼)) = −34 − 94 = −124 = −3
So the expression = 0 + (−3)
3
State the final answer and name the strategy
Commutativity let us reorder; associativity let us regroup into two manageable pairs. The four-term expression simplifies to a single integer.
Strategy used Commutative + associative properties — regroup to make pairs that cancel or share a denominator
Works for any number of terms: spot the "nice" pairs first
Answer −1.8 + (−¾) + 1.8 + (−2¼) = −3
Example 2 — Converting to fraction form for multiplication: calculate (−2.5) × (−⅘)
1
Identify the efficient form — convert 2.5 to a fraction
2.5 is a terminating decimal, so it converts exactly: 2.5 = 52. Writing both factors as fractions will enable cross-cancellation.
(−2.5) × (−45)
Convert 2.5 = 52
= (−52) × (−45)
2
Apply the sign rule — negative × negative = positive
Both factors are negative (same signs), so the product is positive. Handle the sign separately, then multiply the magnitudes.
(−) × (−) = (+)
= 52 × 45   (positive result)
3
Cross-cancel before multiplying — 5 appears in both a numerator and a denominator
The 5 in the first numerator and the 5 in the second denominator cancel to 1. This avoids multiplying large numbers then simplifying afterwards.
52 × 45  →  12 × 41 = 42 = 2
Strategy used Convert decimal → fraction, then cross-cancel before multiplying
Cross-cancellation: divide a numerator and a denominator by the same factor first
Answer (−2.5) × (−⅘) = 2
Example 3 — Multi-operation expression: calculate −¼ + (−0.5) × 6 − (−1½)
1
Order of operations — multiplication before addition or subtraction
BODMAS applies to rational numbers just as to integers. Calculate (−0.5) × 6 first. Decimal form is efficient here: half of 6 is 3, then negate.
(−0.5) × 6 = −3   (half of 6 = 3, negative result)
So: −14 + (−3) − (−1½)
2
Handle the double negative — subtracting a negative means adding
− (−1½) = + 1½. This is a common source of errors: always expand double negatives explicitly before continuing.
−14 + (−3) − (−1½)
= −14 − 3 + 1½   (subtracting a negative = adding)
3
Regroup using associativity — combine integer-adjacent terms first
Pair −3 and +1½ together first (both involve halves, easy to combine). Then add the remaining −¼. Convert to quarters for the final step.
−14 + (−3 + 1½)
= −14 + (−1½)
= −14 − 64   (1½ = 64 in quarters)
= −74 = −1¾
Answer −¼ + (−0.5) × 6 − (−1½) = −74

Efficient strategy toolkit — operations with rational numbers

Regroup to cancel ★ Use commutativity to reorder, associativity to regroup. Spot pairs that cancel to 0 or simplify to integers before computing the rest.
Choose fraction form when… Cross-cancellation is possible, or exact arithmetic is needed. Convert terminating decimals (e.g. 2.5 = 5/2) to unlock cancellation.
Choose decimal form when… Mental arithmetic is straightforward (e.g. 0.5 × 14 = 7 instantly) or place value makes the steps obvious.
Watch for double negatives ★ Subtracting a negative means adding: −(−a) = +a. Expand this explicitly — it's the most common sign error in multi-step problems.
